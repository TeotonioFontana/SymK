# I. Foundational Definitions of Intelligence

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**User:**\
Wher can I find a good definition for intelligency, phylosifically
grounded and with a psyvology basement.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Assistant:**\
You're asking the right way. "Intelligence" is one of those words
everyone uses and almost nobody defines properly.

If you want something **philosophically serious** and **psychologically
grounded**, these are the best starting points.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1) Aristotle -- *Nous* (Intellect)

**Core idea:**\
Intelligence is the faculty that grasps first principles and universal
truths.

He distinguishes: - **Techne** → practical skill\
- **Phronesis** → practical wisdom\
- **Nous** → intellectual intuition

For Aristotle, intelligence is not just processing information. It is
the capacity to apprehend causes and principles.

This gives you metaphysical depth. It answers *what kind of thing
intelligence is*.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2) Immanuel Kant -- Structured Mind

**Core idea:**\
Intelligence is not passive reception. The mind structures experience.

We don't just "detect reality."\
We organize it through categories (causality, unity, plurality, etc.).

This gives you a cognitive architecture view before cognitive science
existed.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3) Jean Piaget -- Adaptive Construction

**Core idea (very solid psychologically):**

> Intelligence is the capacity to adapt to the environment through
> assimilation and accommodation.

This is probably the cleanest psychologically grounded definition.

-   **Assimilation** → fit reality into your model\
-   **Accommodation** → change your model to fit reality

Intelligence = dynamic equilibrium between the two.

This is extremely useful if you're thinking about AI or human learning
models.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4) David Wechsler -- Classic Psychological Definition

He defined intelligence as:

> "The global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think
> rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment."

This is still one of the best operational definitions in psychology.

Notice the three pillars: - Purpose - Rationality - Adaptation

Clean. Elegant. Hard to refute.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5) Howard Gardner -- Multiple Intelligences

He expands the concept beyond IQ:

-   Linguistic\
-   Logical-mathematical\
-   Spatial\
-   Musical\
-   Interpersonal\
-   Intrapersonal\
-   etc.

Controversial, but useful if you want a pluralistic model.

# II. Metaphysical Foundations

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**User:**\
Thought-provoking, let's explore these angles, one-by-one, beginning
with the metaphysical foundation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Assistant:**\
Alright. Metaphysics first: not "how to measure intelligence," but
**what kind of thing it is**.

## 1) The starting fork: *Property* or *Process*?

You can't define intelligence cleanly until you choose this.

### A. Intelligence as a **power/capacity** (a real feature of an agent)

-   Like "seeing" is a capacity of an organism.
-   Intelligence = a **disposition** to reliably produce goal-directed,
    reality-tracking behavior across situations.

**Metaphysical commitment:** capacities are real, not just convenient
labels.

**Upside:** matches how we talk about minds and skills.\
**Downside:** risks sounding vague unless you ground it in mechanisms.

### B. Intelligence as a **process/activity**

-   Intelligence isn't a "thing you have," it's **something you do**:
    model → infer → decide → act → update.
-   More like "running" than "muscle."

**Metaphysical commitment:** intelligence is an **organized pattern of
operations**, not an inner substance.

**Upside:** plays nicely with AI and neuroscience.\
**Downside:** if it's just "a process," you still owe an account of what
makes it *intelligent* rather than mere computation.

My blunt take: **capacity + process** is the least dishonest combo. The
capacity is the stable potential; the process is the actualization.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2) The big metaphysical pillars

### Pillar 1: **Intentionality** (aboutness)

A thermostat reacts; a mind represents. Intelligence involves states
that are *about* the world (even if imperfectly).

Hard question: is "aboutness" fundamental, or can it emerge from
function + learning?

-   If **fundamental**: you're closer to classical philosophy of mind.
-   If **emergent**: you're closer to naturalism / cognitive science.

### Pillar 2: **Teleology** (goal-directedness)

Intelligence isn't random cleverness. It's **organized toward ends**.
Even when goals are implicit (survival, curiosity, coherence).

Key distinction: - **Derived teleology**: artifacts have goals because
we assign them (a calculator "aims" to compute). - **Intrinsic
teleology**: organisms have ends from within (self-maintenance,
reproduction, autonomy).

This matters a lot for AI: is AI's "goal" intrinsic or borrowed?

### Pillar 3: **Rational normativity** (can be right/wrong)

Intelligence is evaluated against norms: - true/false beliefs -
valid/invalid reasoning - better/worse decisions

This is where purely physical descriptions start sweating. Physics tells
you what happens, not what *should* happen. Yet intelligence seems
inseparable from "should."

So any metaphysical foundation must explain normativity: -
**Platonist-ish**: norms are objective. - **Naturalist**: norms reduce
to successful prediction/control. - **Pragmatist**: norms are tools for
coping, not mirrors of reality.
