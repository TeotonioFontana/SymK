# From Properties to Projections

## A SymK Foundation for Metadata, Taxonomy, Classification, and Physical Organization

**Status:** Foundational working paper  
**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 9 August 2026  
**Scope:** Conceptual foundation, methodological proposal, and engineering consequences for the SymK treatment of document organization

---

## Abstract

Systems that manage documents are repeatedly asked to answer a deceptively simple question: *What is the correct classification tree?* The question usually appears when a historical filesystem must be migrated, a SharePoint library must be designed, a knowledge repository must be reorganized, or a controlled vocabulary must be created. It invites teams to begin by drawing folders and naming categories. That beginning is often premature.

This paper develops a SymK method from a different starting point. A document is not its folder, and a classification is not the document itself. Documents and other knowledge objects can be described through multiple properties: type, purpose, subject, author, patient, date, status, jurisdiction, confidentiality, provenance, use pattern, and many others. A classification selects some of those properties, interprets them for a purpose, and projects the objects into categories, sequences, clusters, or navigational structures. Different purposes can therefore produce different useful classifications over the same objects. A physical folder tree is only one implementation of one selected projection under particular technical and operational constraints.

The paper refines this intuition in three ways. First, a property is not identical to a metadata field. Metadata is a governed assertion about a resource: it has a meaning, value, source, status, scope, and sometimes uncertainty. Second, the space generated by properties is only potentially Cartesian. Real property dimensions may be dependent, conditional, multivalued, temporal, unknown, disputed, or inapplicable. Third, purpose-dependence does not make classification arbitrary. The objects, the domain, authoritative standards, user practices, legal constraints, and evidence all resist bad projections. Some classifications are demonstrably better than others for a specified task.

The proposed **Property-First, Projection-Based Classification Method** is iterative. It begins by framing the corpus, community, and purposes; studies both documents and historical practices; discovers candidate properties; governs selected metadata and vocabularies; generates multiple candidate projections; evaluates them against real retrieval and governance tasks; and only then chooses a physical implementation. The resulting system preserves alternate views, provenance, uncertainty, and reversibility.

The medical-document library discussed during the development of the SPServicesAPI Migrator serves as the running example. Its immediate objective is not to construct a universal medical ontology. It is to help physicians quickly find, reproduce, and reprint documents such as prescriptions, exam requests, certificates, reports, referrals, and patient instructions across thousands of historical files. The example shows why a familiar document-type tree may remain a strong primary access path while patient, time, exam modality, clinical purpose, regulatory class, and other dimensions become metadata-supported projections.

The central conclusion is:

> **Objects are described through properties. Metadata governs assertions about those properties. Classifications are purpose-dependent projections over selected properties. Taxonomies organize selected concepts and relations. Physical structures implement selected projections; they do not define the domain.**

## 1. Why classification is a SymK problem

SymK began as a protocol for symbiotic cooperation between Human Intelligence and Artificial Intelligence. It evolved into an Engineering of Knowledge System and generalized its objective toward cooperation among Intelligences of any kind. That evolution makes classification more than a document-management convenience.

Cooperation depends on distinctions. Participants must be able to identify objects, state what they know about them, relate them to other objects, retrieve them for a purpose, and understand why a system placed or presented them in a particular way. Every repository, vocabulary, index, ontology, rule catalog, and knowledge graph therefore makes classificatory choices, whether or not those choices are acknowledged.

The problem extends far beyond the SPServicesAPI Migrator and LexBrain. It appears whenever SymK or a SymK-derived system must organize:

- documents and records;
- claims and evidence;
- concepts and terms;
- agents and capabilities;
- laws, cases, scientific findings, or clinical artifacts;
- models, methods, decisions, and revisions;
- or SymK's own foundational objects.

If the conceptual foundation is weak, technical structures silently inherit several recurring errors:

1. A folder tree is treated as if it were the natural structure of the domain.
2. A label found in filenames is promoted directly into a canonical concept.
3. A metadata field is treated as if it were an objective property without provenance or interpretation.
4. One useful view is mistaken for the only valid taxonomy.
5. Platform restrictions are transformed into ontological claims.
6. Historical user practices are either obeyed mechanically or discarded as mere disorder.
7. Unknown, ambiguous, absent, and inapplicable values are collapsed.
8. Automated inferences become facts because the system has nowhere to store uncertainty.
9. External standards are either ignored or copied without testing their fit to the local purpose.
10. A physical migration becomes irreversible because the evidence from the source arrangement is lost.

SymK needs a universal method that prevents these errors without pretending that every domain should use the same taxonomy. The universal part must concern *how classifications are discovered, justified, implemented, and governed*. The domain categories themselves remain contextual.

## 2. The foundational correction: the tree is not the domain

The ordinary workflow begins with a tree:

```text
Documents
├── Prescriptions
├── Exam Requests
├── Certificates
└── Reports
```

The tree may be useful. It may be familiar, stable, and efficient. The mistake is not using it. The mistake is treating it as if the documents intrinsically arrived in the world with these folder paths attached.

Consider one exam request. It may simultaneously be:

- an `Exam Request` by document type;
- an `Imaging` request by diagnostic modality;
- a request for `Magnetic Resonance Imaging` by examination;
- a document for a particular patient;
- a product of a particular physician or specialty;
- a document issued in a particular year;
- an artifact intended for authorization by a health plan;
- a frequently reused template;
- and a historical file formerly located under a particular folder path.

None of these descriptions is automatically the single true place of the object. Each identifies a dimension that may matter for a particular operation.

The same corpus can therefore support several views:

```text
                         same document collection
                                  |
          +-----------------------+-----------------------+
          |                       |                       |
          v                       v                       v
    by document type        by exam modality          by patient
          |                       |                       |
          v                       v                       v
  Prescriptions/Requests    Imaging/Laboratory      Patient record
```

The tree is a projection: it selects a distinction, orders its categories, and suppresses other dimensions from the visible path. A platform that permits only one primary path forces a choice among views. It does not establish that the chosen view is metaphysically privileged.

### 2.1 Does a true taxonomy exist?

The phrase *true taxonomy* can mean several different things.

In parts of science, a classification may attempt to represent real structures, causal mechanisms, evolutionary lineages, or natural regularities. In law and administration, a classification may be correct because an authoritative rule constitutes the relevant classes within a jurisdiction. In a controlled vocabulary, a term may be preferred because a governing community has adopted it. In an information system, a classification may be better because it improves retrieval for specified users and tasks.

These are different standards of correctness.

For an operational document collection, SymK should not assume that one complete, context-free, uniquely true tree exists. The same document can participate in several legitimate relations. A tree also has representational limits: it privileges hierarchical containment, usually requires a primary parent, and gives a visible order to only a small number of dimensions.

This is not unrestricted relativism. A projection can still be wrong, misleading, unlawful, unusable, or poorly supported. The corpus constrains what may reasonably be asserted. Domain knowledge constrains valid meanings and relationships. Standards may constrain codes and definitions. Users expose retrieval failures. Governance and privacy impose further limits. Purpose-dependent classification is evidence-bound, not arbitrary.

### 2.2 Classification can also shape the object

The cleanest statement is not that objects always exist in a fully formed state before every classification. Some social and institutional objects are partly constituted by classification. A controlled prescription, a legal pleading, a confidential record, or an approved claim has a status created by rules and practices, not only by physical content.

The necessary distinction is this:

> **An object is not exhausted by any one classification assigned to it, even when a classification helps constitute one of its institutional properties.**

This qualification prevents the method from becoming naïvely objectivist. Classifications do not merely mirror a passive world. As Bowker and Star show, classification systems and standards become infrastructure: they affect visibility, work, identity, inclusion, and consequences [Bowker and Star 1999]. SymK must therefore examine not only whether a classification retrieves objects efficiently, but also what the classification causes.

## 3. The vocabulary SymK must keep distinct

SymK cannot build this method without a disciplined vocabulary. The following terms are working distinctions, not yet a ratified ontology.

### 3.1 Object or resource

An **object** is the entity under description or organization. In the present case it may be a file, document, document version, template, record, or logical work. These identities must not be assumed equivalent. One medical prescription may exist as an editable source file, a signed PDF, several copies, and a later revision.

*Resource* is used when emphasizing that the object is described, retrieved, governed, or used within an information system.

### 3.2 Property

A **property** is something that can meaningfully be asserted of, or related to, an object. Examples include document type, creation date, author, patient, purpose, legal status, source path, or relation to another document.

Properties are not all of one kind. A working inventory should distinguish at least:

| Property family | Examples |
|---|---|
| Intrinsic or content-bearing | language, expressed document kind, named examination |
| Technical | file format, size, checksum, encoding |
| Temporal | creation, issue, modification, expiration, migration time |
| Relational | patient, author, referring document, superseded version |
| Contextual | clinical episode, specialty, jurisdiction, intended audience |
| Operational | workflow state, reuse frequency, review queue, print demand |
| Governance | confidentiality, retention rule, authority, access class |
| Historical or provenance | original filename, source path, prior label, source system |
| Derived or inferred | classifier result, normalized name, confidence score |
| Evaluative | quality status, approval, validation outcome |

A property may be single-valued or multivalued, stable or time-dependent, directly observed or inferred, mandatory or optional, private or public.

### 3.3 Property value

A **property value** is a value asserted for a property in relation to an object. Values may be literals, identifiers, dates, quantities, codes, concepts, other objects, or structured records.

`Unknown`, `not collected`, `withheld`, `ambiguous`, and `not applicable` are not synonyms. A robust model must preserve their differences.

### 3.4 Metadata statement

**Metadata** is structured information that supports the description, discovery, use, management, or preservation of a resource [NISO 2004]. For SymK, a metadata statement is not the property itself. It is a governed assertion that an object has a value for a property under some interpretation.

A serious metadata statement may need to preserve:

```text
subject resource
property
value
vocabulary or datatype
source or derivation
asserting intelligence or process
time and applicable scope
confidence or validation status
governance status
```

Thus `document_type = prescription` is incomplete when the value was inferred from a filename with moderate confidence, while the same statement may be authoritative when declared by the document-producing system.

### 3.5 Concept

A **concept** is a unit of meaning recognized within a conceptual system. The same concept may have preferred, alternative, historical, or multilingual labels. Conversely, the same word may refer to different concepts.

SKOS deliberately treats its notion of concept flexibly because knowledge-organization systems vary in practice [W3C 2009a]. SymK should retain that flexibility while requiring identity, scope, provenance, and governance when concepts become operational.

### 3.6 Controlled vocabulary

A **controlled vocabulary** is a governed set of terms or concept labels used consistently for description, indexing, retrieval, or exchange. It may include definitions, preferred and alternative labels, scope notes, codes, and relationships.

A list of strings in source code is not automatically a mature controlled vocabulary. Governance, maintenance, versioning, and declared semantics matter. ISO 25964, ANSI/NISO Z39.19, and SKOS provide relevant established practices [ISO 2011; NISO 2010; W3C 2009a].

### 3.7 Classification scheme and classification

A **classification scheme** defines categories, their meaning and relationships, and the rules or practices by which objects are assigned to them.

**Classification** is the act, process, or result of assigning, grouping, ordering, or distinguishing objects under such a scheme for a stated purpose.

A classification need not be a strict partition. Objects may receive multiple classes, categories may overlap, an object may remain unclassified, and membership may be uncertain or graded.

### 3.8 Taxonomy

A **taxonomy** is a knowledge-organization structure that primarily organizes concepts or categories through hierarchical relations. Real taxonomies may be mono-hierarchical or polyhierarchical and may include selected associative relations.

The word is often used loosely for any category list, folder tree, navigation menu, or ontology. SymK should avoid that collapse. A folder tree can implement a taxonomy, but it can also reflect security, ownership, chronology, or technical partitioning. A taxonomy can exist without any folder structure.

### 3.9 Facet

A **facet** is an explicit dimension of description or classification whose values can be combined with values from other dimensions. Faceted classification, associated historically with Ranganathan and developed further in knowledge-organization research, provides an important precedent for analyzing complex subjects into combinable aspects [Ranganathan 1933; Broughton 2017].

Facets should not be assumed fully independent. `Exam modality` may apply only when `Document type` is `Exam request`; regulatory class may apply only to certain prescriptions. The value of facet analysis is that it exposes dimensions that a single enumerated tree would otherwise fuse together.

### 3.10 Ontology

An **ontology**, in the knowledge-engineering sense, is an explicit specification of a conceptualization: it identifies relevant kinds of entities, relations, and often constraints or axioms for a domain [Gruber 1993]. Ontologies can express more than a taxonomy, including non-hierarchical relations and formal restrictions.

A taxonomy is not automatically an ontology. Metadata fields do not automatically form one either. SymK should introduce formal ontology only when the required reasoning, interoperability, or semantic commitments justify its cost.

### 3.11 View or projection

A **view** is a presentation or access arrangement produced for a particular user, task, or context.

A **projection** is the governed transformation that selects and interprets properties or relations to produce a classification, ordering, grouping, filter, visualization, or view. The term emphasizes that the result reveals some dimensions and suppresses others.

### 3.12 Physical organization

**Physical organization** is the placement of files or records into concrete containers: directories, SharePoint libraries, folders, sites, partitions, or storage locations. It may also encode ownership, access, retention, performance, or lifecycle boundaries.

Physical organization is an implementation decision. It may realize a useful classification, but it must not be confused with the complete conceptual organization of the domain.

## 4. The property space

The user's central intuition can be expressed formally.

Let:

- \(O\) be a set of objects;
- \(P = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\}\) be a set of properties;
- \(V_i\) be the possible value space for property \(p_i\).

In the simplest case, each property is a function:

\[
p_i : O \rightarrow V_i
\]

The combined description of an object would then occupy a point in the product space:

\[
V_1 \times V_2 \times \cdots \times V_n
\]

This formulation captures the insight that a collection may be organized along many combinations of dimensions. A classification by document type uses one projection; a classification by patient and year uses another; a classification by diagnostic modality and clinical purpose uses another.

### 4.1 Why the real space is not a simple Cartesian product

Real metadata requires a richer model.

- Some properties are multivalued.
- Some values are unknown or disputed.
- Some properties apply only under conditions.
- Some values change with time.
- Some statements depend on jurisdiction or context.
- Some properties are inferred rather than observed.
- Some combinations are impossible or nonsensical.
- Some properties depend on other properties.

Therefore, the actual description space is better understood as a constrained subset of the potential product:

\[
D \subseteq V_1 \times V_2 \times \cdots \times V_n
\]

with partial, multivalued, temporal, and provenance-bearing assertions.

The Cartesian-product intuition remains valuable. It explains why properties generate a very large space of possible classifications. It should not be taken to imply that every combination is meaningful or that the dimensions are independent.

### 4.2 Properties do not automatically become metadata

A corpus may expose hundreds of detectable properties. Storing all of them would create cost, ambiguity, privacy risk, and governance burden. The question is not only *What can be extracted?* It is:

- Which property supports a real task?
- Is its meaning stable enough to govern?
- Can its value be obtained accurately?
- Is it worth the cost of collection and maintenance?
- Does it expose sensitive information?
- Is it authoritative, observed, inferred, or derived?
- Will it remain interpretable after migration?
- Does it belong in metadata, full text, an audit record, or nowhere?

The method is therefore **property-first**, not **metadata-maximalist**.

### 4.3 Formal Concept Analysis as a neighboring model

Formal Concept Analysis begins from a relation between objects and attributes and derives formal concepts whose extensions contain objects and whose intensions contain shared attributes. These concepts form a lattice rather than necessarily a single tree [Ganter and Wille 1999].

This is a powerful mathematical neighbor to the SymK proposal. It demonstrates rigorously that object-attribute relations can generate multiple conceptual groupings and ordered structures. SymK's method is not identical to Formal Concept Analysis, however. SymK must also handle multivalued and temporal properties, institutional categories, uncertainty, authority, user purpose, privacy, physical implementation, and consequences. Formal derivation is one possible analytical instrument within the method, not its universal definition.

### 4.4 Identity must precede description

Before comparing property values, the system must know what counts as the same object. A filesystem file, a logical document, a revision, a signed rendition, a duplicate, and a template may have different identities and relations.

Without an identity policy, metadata can accidentally combine different objects or multiply one object into many independent records. Property discovery therefore depends on an earlier question:

> **What exactly is the object being described, and under which identity and version rules?**

## 5. Classification as projection

For a purpose \(u\), a classification projection may be represented abstractly as:

\[
C_u = f_u(O, P_u, R_u, X_u)
\]

where:

- \(P_u\) is the selected subset of properties;
- \(R_u\) is the set of interpretation, normalization, and assignment rules;
- \(X_u\) is the context, including users, tasks, authority, time, and constraints;
- \(C_u\) is the resulting classification or access structure.

The formula makes four important points.

First, the projection is **selective**. It uses only some properties. Second, it is **interpretive**. Raw values do not group themselves; rules and concepts supply meaning. Third, it is **contextual**. A view useful for reprinting documents may differ from one useful for retention review. Fourth, it is **revisable**. New evidence, tasks, or concepts can justify a different projection.

### 5.1 Useful and useless projections

The property space permits many possible groupings, but possibility does not imply usefulness. Documents could be grouped by the parity of their byte size or the first hexadecimal digit of their checksum. These are valid computations but usually useless classifications for a physician.

Usefulness depends on a relationship among:

```text
objects + users + tasks + context + cost + consequences
```

A good projection makes distinctions that support the intended work. It should improve finding, understanding, deciding, governing, or acting enough to justify the complexity it introduces.

### 5.2 A projection does not need to be a tree

A projection may produce:

- a hierarchy or polyhierarchy;
- a faceted navigation interface;
- a sequence or timeline;
- a matrix;
- a graph of relations;
- a cluster discovered from observed similarities;
- a saved query;
- a search ranking;
- a security or retention partition;
- or a dashboard.

The tree is only one output form. It is often excellent for stable, familiar, mutually understandable categories. It becomes harmful when forced to represent every dimension simultaneously.

### 5.3 Multiple projections can coexist without conceptual contradiction

A prescription can appear in a type-based view, a patient view, a yearly view, and a regulated-document view without being copied into four independent conceptual objects. Metadata and relations allow several projections over one governed identity.

This multiplicity is not duplicate truth. It is controlled plurality of access.

### 5.4 Projection differs from arbitrary perspective

SymK should evaluate a projection by evidence. A projection must declare:

- the purpose it serves;
- the properties and concepts it uses;
- the rules that produce membership or ordering;
- the population and time for which it applies;
- the authority or evidence behind its meanings;
- its known exclusions and failure modes;
- and the consequences of using it.

Two projections can both be legitimate for different purposes. One can also be clearly inferior for the same purpose when it produces more retrieval failures, higher assignment ambiguity, greater privacy risk, or worse maintenance cost.

## 6. What the established traditions contribute

SymK is not inventing classification from nothing. Several mature traditions illuminate different parts of the problem. None alone supplies the whole method.

### 6.1 Library classification and facet analysis

Enumerative classification attempts to list compound subjects in advance. Facet analysis decomposes them into dimensions that can be synthesized. Ranganathan's *Colon Classification* was a decisive early implementation, and later work generalized facet analysis as a method for knowledge organization and retrieval [Ranganathan 1933; Broughton 2017; Raghavan 2024].

The contribution to SymK is fundamental: a complex object need not be assigned only one pre-enumerated label. Independent aspects can be represented separately and recombined for navigation.

SymK should not copy Ranganathan's particular canonical facets as universal primitives. The lesson is methodological, not mechanical. Each domain must discover which facets are meaningful, how they depend on one another, and which combinations support use.

### 6.2 Metadata standards

The Dublin Core Metadata Initiative demonstrates how a small, governed vocabulary can describe resources across implementations [DCMI 2020]. NISO distinguishes descriptive, structural, and administrative roles for metadata and emphasizes discovery, management, use, and preservation [NISO 2004].

These traditions show that metadata is broader than subject classification. File format, rights, provenance, structure, and preservation information may be essential without belonging to a taxonomy.

### 6.3 Controlled vocabularies and SKOS

ISO 25964 and ANSI/NISO Z39.19 describe principles for constructing, maintaining, and using controlled vocabularies and thesauri [ISO 2011; NISO 2010]. SKOS provides a common, deliberately lightweight model for representing concepts, preferred and alternative labels, broader and narrower relations, associative relations, notes, concept schemes, and mappings among schemes [W3C 2009a; W3C 2009b].

The contribution is both semantic and governmental. Labels change; synonyms occur; concepts require scope notes; external schemes need mappings; deprecated terms must remain interpretable. A YAML alias list can begin this work, but it does not complete it.

### 6.4 Formal Concept Analysis

Formal Concept Analysis supplies a mathematically disciplined way to examine how shared attributes induce concepts and a lattice of relations [Ganter and Wille 1999]. It can help discover implicational structure, redundancies, and possible groupings in an empirical corpus.

Its contribution is strongest as an analytical lens. It does not itself determine which concepts a community should govern, which projections serve a task, or which consequences are acceptable.

### 6.5 Ontology engineering

Ontology engineering makes conceptual commitments explicit and can support interoperability and reasoning [Gruber 1993]. It is valuable when the system must distinguish document, patient, author, clinical event, examination, template, rendition, and relationships among them.

Its caution is equally valuable: formalization commits the system to meanings and constraints. Premature ontology can freeze uncertain distinctions behind technical precision. SymK should formalize only after the conceptual burden is understood.

### 6.6 Critical classification studies

Bowker and Star show that categories and standards are embedded in infrastructures and have material consequences [Bowker and Star 1999]. Hjørland's review of classification emphasizes that classifications depend on theories, purposes, and perspectives rather than emerging from neutral resemblance alone [Hjørland 2017].

This tradition prevents SymK from treating classification as a harmless rearrangement. A category can expose or conceal a population, grant or deny access, change workload, create stigma, determine retention, or establish what an institution can count.

### 6.7 The SymK synthesis

These traditions can be placed together without collapsing them:

| Tradition | Primary contribution | What it does not decide alone |
|---|---|---|
| Facet analysis | Decomposition into combinable dimensions | Which dimensions matter for this community |
| Metadata standards | Governed resource description | The best domain classification |
| Controlled vocabularies and SKOS | Concept labels, semantic relations, mappings, maintenance | The truth or utility of every local category |
| Formal Concept Analysis | Structures derivable from object-attribute relations | Purpose, authority, privacy, and social consequence |
| Ontology engineering | Explicit concepts, relations, and constraints | Whether formalization is mature or worth its cost |
| Critical classification studies | Consequences, power, history, invisibility | The detailed implementation for a given corpus |

The SymK method connects these contributions through explicit purpose, evidence, governance, and revision.

## 7. The Property-First, Projection-Based Classification Method

The proposed method is iterative. Its sequence is logical, not a rigid waterfall. Later stages can reveal missing properties, mistaken identities, weak concepts, or misunderstood tasks and send the work back to earlier stages.

```text
Frame -> Observe -> Discover -> Govern -> Project -> Evaluate -> Implement
  ^                                                               |
  +---------------- Observe use, revise, and preserve history <----+
```

### Stage 0 — Frame the domain, corpus, community, and purpose

Before examining categories, state:

- What objects are in scope?
- What objects are explicitly outside it?
- Who creates, maintains, finds, uses, and governs them?
- Which tasks must become faster, safer, or more reliable?
- What legal, ethical, security, retention, and technical constraints apply?
- What outcome would demonstrate improvement?

This stage prevents a classification designed for one lifecycle from being imposed on another. In the medical-library example, locally produced documents intended for reproduction and reprinting form one operational corpus. Laboratory results maintained by external laboratories or practice-management systems belong to a different moment, lifecycle, authority, and use pattern. Their topical similarity does not require one library.

### Stage 1 — Establish object identity and boundaries

Decide what counts as:

- one logical document;
- a version or rendition;
- a duplicate;
- a template and an instance;
- an attachment or compound document;
- a deleted, superseded, or historical object.

Identity errors contaminate every later property and statistic.

### Stage 2 — Observe the corpus and the work

Study more than filenames. Evidence can include:

- file contents and technical properties;
- directory paths and naming patterns;
- document templates and repeated structures;
- existing database fields;
- search queries and navigation behavior;
- reprint and reuse patterns;
- user interviews and work observation;
- support requests and retrieval failures;
- legal and professional standards;
- authoritative external vocabularies;
- exceptions, unknowns, and abandoned conventions.

This is both corpus analysis and practice analysis. A classification is intended for people performing work, not for an abstract set of files alone.

### Stage 3 — Discover candidate properties

Create a property inventory without immediately forcing it into a tree. For each candidate property, record:

| Question | Purpose |
|---|---|
| What does it mean? | Prevents label-only semantics |
| To which object type does it apply? | Prevents invalid universal fields |
| What is its value domain? | Supports validation and interoperability |
| Is it intrinsic, relational, contextual, operational, or governance-related? | Clarifies use and ownership |
| Is it observed, declared, inferred, or derived? | Preserves epistemic status |
| Is it stable or temporal? | Prevents silent overwriting |
| Is it single- or multivalued? | Prevents forced loss |
| Who is authoritative? | Supports conflict resolution |
| How costly is collection and maintenance? | Controls metadata burden |
| Is it sensitive? | Supports privacy and access design |
| Which tasks could it support? | Connects description to value |

Property discovery is exploratory. It does not commit the system to storing every candidate.

### Stage 4 — Recover historical semantics

Treat the source filesystem as evidence. Folder names, prefixes, abbreviations, repeated filenames, and placement patterns reveal:

- familiar distinctions;
- local terminology;
- chronological layers;
- individual workarounds;
- former system limitations;
- implicit workflows;
- and unresolved ambiguity.

This activity may be called **semantic archaeology**. The historical tree is neither truth nor noise. It is a record of how users and systems previously negotiated the collection.

Preserve the original path and filename as provenance even if the target arrangement changes. Without them, a migration destroys evidence that may be needed for audit, correction, or future interpretation.

### Stage 5 — Select and govern metadata

Choose the candidate properties worth representing. Selection criteria include task value, reliability, stability, privacy, interoperability, automation potential, and cost.

For each selected property define:

- identifier and human-readable label;
- definition and scope note;
- object types to which it applies;
- datatype or controlled value scheme;
- cardinality;
- required, recommended, or optional status;
- source and authority rules;
- unknown and inapplicable handling;
- validation rules;
- security and retention requirements;
- version and change policy.

At this stage, raw filename tokens may become aliases for governed concepts, but they should not automatically become preferred terms.

### Stage 6 — Profile evidence quality and gaps

Measure how well the corpus supports the selected metadata:

- coverage by property;
- rate of conflicting values;
- ambiguity and unknown rates;
- distribution and cardinality;
- value drift over time;
- extraction confidence;
- dependence on source path or filename;
- manual review burden;
- sensitive-data exposure.

This stage prevents an elegant classification from depending on metadata that the historical corpus cannot reliably supply.

### Stage 7 — Generate candidate projections

Create several possible classifications and views. Each candidate should declare:

- target users and tasks;
- selected properties;
- concept scheme and assignment rules;
- whether membership is exclusive, overlapping, hierarchical, faceted, or ranked;
- expected unknown and ambiguous cases;
- physical and virtual presentation options;
- and consequences of error.

Possible projections may come from domain expertise, observed habits, facet analysis, external standards, Formal Concept Analysis, clustering, query logs, or combinations of these. Machine-discovered clusters remain hypotheses until interpreted and evaluated.

### Stage 8 — Evaluate projections against real tasks

A candidate projection should be tested, not admired. Relevant criteria include:

| Criterion | Evaluation question |
|---|---|
| Task relevance | Does it support the actual decisions and retrieval actions? |
| Cognitive fit | Do users understand where to look and why? |
| Familiarity | Does it preserve valuable learned practice? |
| Coverage | How much of the corpus can it represent? |
| Discriminability | Do categories make useful distinctions without excessive overlap? |
| Stability | Will the distinctions survive ordinary change? |
| Metadata feasibility | Can required values be obtained and maintained? |
| Assignment reliability | Can people and machines apply it consistently? |
| Retrieval performance | Does it improve findability, time, precision, or recall? |
| Governance fit | Can owners define, review, and revise it? |
| Privacy and security | Does the view reveal or combine sensitive data improperly? |
| Interoperability | Can it map to relevant standards and systems? |
| Consequences | Who gains visibility, workload, authority, or risk? |
| Reversibility | Can the decision be changed without destroying evidence? |
| Technical fit | Can the platform implement it without distorting its meaning? |

Evaluation should include representative users and difficult cases, not only clean examples.

### Stage 9 — Choose a primary access projection

A system may need one default view even when several projections are valid. The primary projection is not declared the true taxonomy. It is selected as the best default for the dominant tasks under current constraints.

The decision should be recorded with:

- alternatives considered;
- evidence and task results;
- assumptions;
- known compromises;
- properties deliberately excluded from the physical path;
- and a revision trigger.

### Stage 10 — Design the physical implementation

Only now decide how concepts and metadata map to:

- sites and libraries;
- folders and paths;
- document or content types;
- columns and managed terms;
- search indexes;
- saved views and filters;
- security scopes;
- retention and lifecycle controls;
- migration rules and reports.

The platform should implement the conceptual decision. Its limitations should be documented as constraints, not transformed into domain truth.

### Stage 11 — Migrate with provenance, uncertainty, and review

During migration:

- preserve original identity evidence;
- distinguish declared, observed, inferred, and derived metadata;
- retain confidence and rule provenance;
- route unknown and ambiguous cases explicitly;
- avoid forcing uncertain values into authoritative fields;
- produce an auditable report;
- support dry runs and repeatability;
- and prevent physical placement from erasing alternate classifications.

### Stage 12 — Observe, govern, and revise

After deployment, measure search behavior, failed retrievals, empty categories, overloaded categories, user corrections, vocabulary drift, and new document practices.

Classification is not a one-time act. Concepts, objects, users, and tasks change. The method completes a cycle only when revision is possible.

## 8. Choosing a physical structure

The need for one physical tree is an engineering decision point, not a refutation of plural classification.

### 8.1 What belongs in the primary path?

A good primary path usually relies on distinctions that are:

- frequently used in navigation;
- stable over the object's lifecycle;
- easy to assign with low ambiguity;
- familiar to the community;
- meaningful at each level;
- compatible with access and retention boundaries;
- and not excessively deep or sparse.

Properties that are volatile, multivalued, highly sensitive, or useful mainly for occasional filtering are often better represented as metadata and views rather than nested folders.

### 8.2 When separate physical containers are justified

Different libraries or storage boundaries may be justified when collections have materially different:

- ownership or stewardship;
- access-control requirements;
- retention schedules;
- legal authority;
- lifecycle and workflow;
- scale or performance requirements;
- integration boundaries;
- or disaster-recovery policies.

Topic alone is not always sufficient. Conversely, two topically similar objects may belong in different containers because their governance and lifecycle differ, as with locally produced exam requests and externally maintained exam results.

### 8.3 What folders should not be asked to do

Folders should not be the only store of meaning. If `Imaging/2026/Patient Name` is the sole representation of modality, year, and patient, then moving the file changes its apparent meaning and alternate retrieval depends on parsing a path.

Folders should also not encode every combination of facets. Doing so creates combinatorial growth, inconsistent placement, duplication, and disagreement over which dimension comes first.

### 8.4 The physical-home function is restrictive

A traditional filesystem normally assigns each file one physical home:

\[
h : O \rightarrow \text{one container path}
\]

This function is much more restrictive than the conceptual relations surrounding an object. Metadata, indexes, and views restore some of the suppressed dimensions without requiring physical duplication.

### 8.5 Reversibility is a design quality

Because the selected tree is contingent, the system should make change affordable. Reversibility requires:

- stable object identifiers;
- preserved original paths and filenames;
- explicit classification rules and versions;
- metadata independent of folder names;
- mapping tables among old and new concepts;
- audit logs and migration manifests;
- repeatable dry runs;
- and views that can be rebuilt without moving every file.

The ability to revise a classification is not evidence of weak design. It is evidence that the system understands classification as a governed hypothesis rather than an irreversible truth.

## 9. Running example: the medical-document library

The medical-library problem provides a concrete test because it has a clear operational objective and several plausible classifications.

### 9.1 Operational scope

The collection is intended primarily to help physicians quickly reproduce and reprint documents that patients lose or ask about later. The expected corpus includes artifacts such as:

- simple prescriptions;
- controlled prescriptions;
- high-cost or special-authorization prescriptions;
- exam requests;
- medical certificates;
- attendance declarations;
- medical reports;
- referrals;
- post-consultation instructions;
- and possibly other locally produced documents with the same operational lifecycle.

Exam results are excluded from the initial library. They are normally maintained online by laboratories or practice-management systems and belong to a different clinical moment, source authority, lifecycle, and access pattern. This is a scope decision grounded in work, not a claim that requests and results are semantically unrelated.

### 9.2 Candidate property inventory

A first property inventory might include:

| Property | Example values | Likely role |
|---|---|---|
| Logical document identity | stable identifier | identity and deduplication |
| Document type | prescription, exam request, certificate | primary navigation candidate |
| Document subtype | controlled prescription, attendance declaration | refinement and filtering |
| Patient | patient identifier | retrieval and governance |
| Issuing physician | practitioner identifier | provenance and filtering |
| Specialty | cardiology, pulmonology | context |
| Issue date | date | chronology and validity |
| Clinical purpose | diagnosis, monitoring, authorization | alternate view |
| Exam modality | clinical analysis, imaging, functional | exam-request facet |
| Requested examination | blood count, MRI, ECG | detailed content facet |
| Regulatory class | applicable prescription class | compliance and governance |
| Status | draft, issued, superseded, expired | lifecycle |
| Template relation | based on template X | reproduction workflow |
| Original filename and path | source values | provenance and semantic archaeology |
| Classification evidence | alias, content pattern, source folder | audit |
| Classification confidence | value and method | review routing |
| Reuse or reprint pattern | frequency or last use | operational optimization |
| Confidentiality and access | governed value | protection |

This is an exploratory inventory, not a final SharePoint schema. Each property still requires purpose, authority, cost, privacy, and data-quality evaluation.

### 9.3 Exam modality illustrates conditional facets

The classification of requested examinations may include dimensions such as:

- clinical laboratory analysis;
- diagnostic imaging;
- pathology and cytology;
- genetics;
- functional or cardiological diagnosis;
- neurological diagnosis;
- respiratory diagnosis.

These values can enrich `Exam request`, but they need not become top-level folders for the whole library. `Exam modality` is conditional: it primarily applies when the object is an exam request. One request may also contain several laboratory examinations, while imaging requests may be separated because of scheduling, provider availability, or authorization practices.

The system should therefore distinguish at least:

1. the document object;
2. its type as an exam request;
3. one or more requested examinations;
4. the modality or modalities of those examinations;
5. the operational decision to keep examinations in one document or several.

A filename alone may blur all five.

### 9.4 Candidate projections

The same library supports several legitimate projections:

| Projection | Main use | Limitation as the only tree |
|---|---|---|
| By document type | Familiar reproduction and reprint workflow | Suppresses patient, time, and clinical purpose |
| By patient | Recovering all documents for one patient | Creates privacy, identity, and scale concerns |
| By issue date | Chronological review and lifecycle | Weak semantic navigation |
| By exam modality | Reusing or locating similar requests | Applies only to part of the corpus |
| By clinical purpose | Understanding why documents were produced | Purpose may be difficult to infer reliably |
| By physician or specialty | Locating author-specific patterns and templates | Personnel and responsibilities change |
| By regulatory class | Compliance and controlled-document handling | Too narrow for general navigation |
| By reprint frequency | Operational optimization | Volatile and unsuitable as stable physical placement |

The existing document-type tree may remain the strongest primary projection because users recognize it and the dominant task is document reproduction. That conclusion must be tested against the corpus and user work; it should not be assumed merely because the historical tree exists.

### 9.5 A possible implementation hypothesis

A reasonable hypothesis for evaluation is:

```text
Primary physical or navigational projection
    document type -> selected stable subtype

Metadata-supported alternate projections
    patient | date | physician | exam modality | examination
    clinical purpose | regulatory class | status | provenance
```

This hypothesis preserves a simple learned path while avoiding the attempt to make folders represent every dimension. It is an experimental design candidate, not yet the final taxonomy.

### 9.6 What success means

The library succeeds if physicians and authorized staff can rapidly locate the right reusable or reprintable document with fewer errors and less dependence on remembered filenames or personal folder habits.

Relevant measures could include:

- time to locate a known document;
- success rate for representative retrieval tasks;
- rate of wrong-patient or wrong-version selection;
- number of items requiring manual classification;
- correction rate for inferred metadata;
- user reliance on search versus browsing;
- unresolved unknown and ambiguous rates;
- and maintenance effort after new document types appear.

No taxonomy should be considered successful only because its diagram appears orderly.

## 10. Consequences for the SPServicesAPI Migrator

The Migrator is not merely a file copier once it begins extracting meaning and selecting destinations. It becomes an epistemic and classificatory instrument. Its outputs can harden uncertain interpretations into durable infrastructure.

### 10.1 Separate four responsibilities

The Migrator should keep distinct:

1. **Preservation and filename safety** — copying files, sanitizing names, resolving duplicates, and preserving originals.
2. **Observation and extraction** — detecting tokens, paths, technical attributes, and content evidence.
3. **Classification and metadata inference** — applying governed rules to propose concepts and values.
4. **Physical placement** — implementing the selected primary projection in destination paths or libraries.

Combining these into one hidden rule makes conceptual revision difficult. A new taxonomy should not require rewriting SharePoint filename sanitization. A changed physical structure should not erase classification evidence.

### 10.2 Preserve the evidence chain

For every inferred value, the Migrator should be able to report:

- source object identity;
- original path and filename;
- observed evidence;
- matched alias or pattern;
- rule identifier and version;
- proposed concept identifier;
- confidence or decision status;
- destination decision;
- warnings, conflicts, and human corrections.

The migration report is therefore part of the knowledge infrastructure, not disposable logging.

### 10.3 Unknown is a legitimate result

An `Unknown` route is conceptually important. It prevents forced classification from manufacturing certainty. The system should eventually distinguish:

- no relevant evidence found;
- conflicting evidence;
- candidate values below threshold;
- property not applicable;
- unsupported document type;
- and processing failure.

These cases may share a review queue without sharing a meaning.

### 10.4 Human review produces new knowledge

Corrections should not merely change one destination. They can reveal:

- missing aliases;
- unclear scope notes;
- an absent subtype;
- a property dependency;
- a historical convention;
- or a flaw in the chosen projection.

The system should preserve corrections as governed evidence for vocabulary and rule evolution. It must also avoid naïvely learning from one accidental correction as if it were a universal rule.

### 10.5 Taxonomy rules need their own lifecycle

Rule catalogs and controlled vocabularies should have:

- stable identifiers;
- versions and effective dates;
- preferred and alternative labels;
- scope notes;
- mappings from deprecated values;
- validation before execution;
- owners and review processes;
- test corpora and expected results;
- and compatibility statements for migration manifests.

This is the conceptual reason Sprint 2 moved toward a modular, rule-driven engine. Modularity is not only cleaner code; it protects distinct kinds of meaning from becoming inseparable.

## 11. Candidate foundational commitments

The following statements are candidates for future SymK ratification. They are intentionally stronger than ordinary design advice but remain open to revision.

### CL-01 — Object/classification non-identity

> An object is not identical to any one classification assigned to it, even when a classification helps constitute one of its institutional properties.

This prevents a physical or conceptual category from exhausting the object.

### CL-02 — Property/metadata distinction

> Metadata is a governed assertion about a property of a resource, not the property itself.

Metadata must preserve meaning, value, scope, source, and epistemic status in proportion to consequence.

### CL-03 — Purpose declaration

> Every operational classification must declare the users, tasks, context, and consequences for which it is intended.

There is no context-free guarantee that one classification is best.

### CL-04 — Projection principle

> A classification is a selective and governed projection over properties, relations, concepts, evidence, and context.

It reveals some distinctions and suppresses others.

### CL-05 — Controlled plurality

> Multiple useful projections over the same governed objects may coexist without conceptual contradiction.

Plurality must remain explicit, interpretable, and testable rather than becoming uncontrolled duplication.

### CL-06 — Evidence-bound non-arbitrariness

> Purpose-dependence does not make classification arbitrary.

Objects, domain knowledge, authoritative rules, user tasks, observed performance, and consequences constrain acceptable projections.

### CL-07 — Physical-implementation distinction

> Physical containment is an implementation of selected organizational decisions; it is not the ontology of the domain.

Platform limitations may constrain implementation but must not silently redefine concepts.

### CL-08 — Historical-evidence principle

> A historical filesystem is evidence of prior concepts, practices, constraints, and adaptations; it is neither final authority nor meaningless residue.

Its paths and labels should be preserved as provenance through migration.

### CL-09 — Epistemic-status preservation

> Declared, observed, inferred, derived, disputed, unknown, and inapplicable metadata must not be silently collapsed.

Automated classification must preserve its evidence, method, and confidence.

### CL-10 — Vocabulary governance

> Operational concepts and controlled values require stable identity, definition, ownership, versioning, mapping, and change history.

Labels are interfaces to concepts, not substitutes for them.

### CL-11 — Consequence review

> A classification must be evaluated by what it makes visible, retrievable, permitted, burdensome, excluded, or irreversible—not only by structural elegance.

Classification is an intervention in work and governance.

### CL-12 — Reversibility and alternate access

> When no unique projection is constitutionally required, SymK systems should preserve the evidence and metadata needed to construct alternate views and revise physical organization.

Reversibility protects future knowledge from current constraints.

### CL-13 — Minimum sufficient metadata

> A property should become maintained metadata only when its expected contribution to use, governance, interoperability, or learning justifies its semantic, operational, and privacy cost.

Property-first analysis does not license unlimited metadata collection.

### CL-14 — Iterative validation

> Classification design remains provisional until tested against representative objects, difficult cases, real users, and real tasks, and it remains revisable after deployment.

A tidy tree is not validation.

## 12. Development requirements derived from the foundation

If the candidate commitments survive review, they imply concrete requirements for SymK and derived systems.

### 12.1 Identity must be first-class

Systems must distinguish logical object, file, version, rendition, copy, duplicate, and template relations. Classification should attach to the correct identity level.

### 12.2 Metadata assertions need provenance

High-consequence or inferred metadata should support source, method, time, rule version, confidence, validation status, and responsible actor or process.

### 12.3 Concepts need stable identifiers

Preferred labels may change without changing concept identity. Deprecated concepts need mappings and history. Multilingual or local labels should not create accidental duplicate concepts.

### 12.4 Property applicability must be modeled

Conditional properties should apply to relevant object types or contexts. The absence of `exam modality` on a prescription is not missing data.

### 12.5 Views should be independent from physical paths

Search, filters, saved views, and generated navigation should use governed metadata or relations rather than relying only on folder parsing.

### 12.6 The primary projection must be declared

Systems should record which projection controls default navigation or physical placement, why it was selected, and what alternate projections remain available.

### 12.7 Automated classification must be challengeable

Users need a way to inspect why an object received a value, correct it, and distinguish correction of the object from correction of a general rule.

### 12.8 Unknown and ambiguity need typed states

The model should distinguish absence of evidence, conflict, low confidence, inapplicability, and technical failure. Review queues can aggregate them operationally while preserving semantics.

### 12.9 External schemes should be mapped, not swallowed

Authoritative standards should retain source scheme, version, code, and mapping relation. A local concept may be exact, broader, narrower, or merely related to an external concept. SKOS mapping relations provide one useful starting vocabulary [W3C 2009a].

### 12.10 Governance must include observed use

Vocabulary review should consider query logs, corrections, navigation failures, new document practices, and stakeholder consequences. Governance is not only editorial approval.

### 12.11 Migration must remain repeatable

Dry runs, deterministic rules where possible, manifests, reports, checksums, source preservation, and versioned configurations are required so that conceptual changes can be tested safely.

### 12.12 Privacy must constrain property discovery

The fact that a property can be extracted does not authorize its collection, aggregation, display, or use. Purpose, minimization, access, retention, and legal basis must be evaluated before operationalization.

## 13. Open foundational questions

This paper establishes a method, not a finished universal theory. Several questions require further SymK work.

### 13.1 What is the SymK primitive: property, relation, or assertion?

Many formalisms treat a property as a relation between a subject and value. SymK must decide whether `Property` is primitive or whether governed assertions and relations provide the more minimal foundation.

### 13.2 How should intrinsic and socially constituted properties differ?

File checksum, named patient, controlled-prescription status, and clinical purpose have different dependence on observation, authority, and institutions. One generic field model may hide these differences.

### 13.3 When should a taxonomy become an ontology?

Formal axioms can improve validation and interoperability but increase commitment and maintenance cost. SymK needs a maturity gate for moving from labels and hierarchies to richer formal semantics.

### 13.4 How should a primary projection be selected quantitatively?

Retrieval time, success rate, assignment reliability, cognitive load, privacy risk, stability, and governance cost are heterogeneous. SymK may need a multi-criteria decision model rather than a single score.

### 13.5 How can metadata explosion be prevented?

The property space can generate more potentially useful dimensions than a community can responsibly maintain. The minimum-sufficient-metadata commitment needs measurable thresholds and lifecycle rules.

### 13.6 How should classifications change over time?

Concepts split, merge, change names, or change scope. Objects may need historically correct classifications as well as current ones. SymK must distinguish retrospective reclassification from preservation of prior meaning.

### 13.7 How should machine-discovered structure enter governance?

Clusters, embeddings, and learned classifiers may reveal patterns absent from current vocabularies. SymK needs rules for converting such patterns into proposed concepts without treating statistical proximity as semantic truth.

### 13.8 What counts as authoritative?

Professional standards, law, local practice, users, source systems, and domain experts can conflict. Authority may vary by property. SymK needs property-level authority and conflict-resolution models.

### 13.9 How should classification consequences be represented?

The system can record purpose and provenance, but effects such as exclusion, workload, or stigmatization may emerge only after deployment. Consequence monitoring may require its own knowledge objects and review processes.

### 13.10 Can physical location become semantically neutral?

Stable identifiers, metadata, and strong search may reduce dependence on folders, but users still interpret paths and containers. SymK must determine when physical organization can be treated as operational rather than meaning-bearing.

### 13.11 How should cross-domain projections work?

LexBrain, SPServices, scientific repositories, and medical libraries will have different vocabularies. SymK needs to distinguish universal method, shared upper concepts, domain schemes, and local application profiles.

### 13.12 What makes a projection explainable?

Listing matched rules may not explain why a projection is appropriate. SymK may need explanations at three levels: the object's assignment, the scheme's conceptual rationale, and the institution's choice of that scheme as primary.

## 14. A practical evaluation gate

Before a taxonomy, classification, metadata property, or physical tree is adopted, reviewers should ask:

| Gate | Question |
|---|---|
| Corpus | Which objects and lifecycles are in scope, and which are excluded? |
| Identity | What counts as one object, version, rendition, copy, or template? |
| Community | Who creates, finds, uses, governs, and is affected by the objects? |
| Purpose | Which concrete tasks should the organization improve? |
| Property basis | Which properties and relations support the proposal? |
| Metadata value | Why should each selected property be collected and maintained? |
| Evidence | Are values declared, observed, inferred, derived, or disputed? |
| Vocabulary | Are concepts identified, defined, scoped, versioned, and mapped? |
| Alternatives | Which other projections were considered? |
| Task validation | How did the proposal perform on representative and difficult cases? |
| Historical fit | What useful prior practices are preserved, and what constraints are intentionally abandoned? |
| Consequences | What becomes visible, hidden, permitted, excluded, or burdensome? |
| Privacy and governance | Are collection, access, retention, and authority justified? |
| Physical fit | Does the platform implement the model without redefining it? |
| Uncertainty | How are unknown, ambiguous, conflicting, and inapplicable cases handled? |
| Reversibility | Can the organization change without losing identity, metadata, or source evidence? |
| Stewardship | Who monitors use, resolves disputes, and approves revisions? |

Passing this gate does not prove that a classification is universally true. It establishes that the projection is explicit, evidence-bearing, purpose-tested, governable, and revisable.

## 15. Conclusion

The original question appeared to be about choosing a document taxonomy. The deeper problem is the relationship among objects, properties, metadata, classifications, taxonomies, views, and physical containers.

The correction developed in this paper is simple to state but far-reaching:

> **The physical tree must come last conceptually, even when a familiar tree remains the best practical default.**

The method begins by defining the corpus, object identities, community, work, and constraints. It then studies the documents and their historical organization, discovers candidate properties, selects and governs useful metadata, and develops multiple candidate projections. Those projections are tested against real tasks and consequences. Only then is one chosen as the primary access structure and implemented through the available platform.

This sequence is iterative rather than linear. A failed view may reveal a missing property. An ambiguous property may reveal an unstable concept. A correction may expose a historical convention. A physical limitation may require an explicit compromise. Governance preserves these discoveries so the system can evolve rather than merely accumulate patches.

The user's Cartesian intuition survives in a qualified form. A set of properties generates a large space of possible classifications. The actual corpus occupies only a constrained portion of that space, and only some projections are useful. Usefulness is determined not by preference alone, but by the relation among evidence, purpose, users, authoritative constraints, operational cost, and consequence.

For the medical-document library, this means that document type, patient, date, clinical purpose, exam modality, regulatory class, provenance, and reuse pattern should first be understood as distinct properties or relations. A familiar document-type structure may prove to be the best primary path for rapid reproduction and reprinting. If so, it should be selected because it performs that task well—not because SharePoint permits one tree and therefore one taxonomy must be true. Other dimensions can remain available through governed metadata, views, search, and future projections.

For SymK, the broader result is a reusable constitutional method:

> **Objects are described through properties. Metadata governs assertions about those properties. Classifications are purpose-dependent projections over selected properties. Taxonomies organize selected concepts and relations. Physical structures implement selected projections; they do not define the domain.**

This method permits plural views without surrendering rigor, respects historical practice without making it absolute, uses standards without copying them mechanically, and turns technical reversibility into a form of epistemic responsibility.
