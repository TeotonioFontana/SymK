## 1. Question and analytical result

> How must SymK distinguish event, behavior, output, action, omission, causal Contribution, agency, autonomy, influence, and control before any responsibility or authority can be allocated?

Stream A supports the following working answer:

> **Agency is a scoped capability of an organized subject to govern conduct through integrated selection and control processes that respond systematically to relevant variation, such that materially different conduct remains attributable to the subject under materially different conditions.**

> **An action is an event or process attributable, within a declared target and Scope, to an exercise of agency. Causal Contribution is a model-relative difference made to an event, condition, action, omission, or consequence; it does not by itself establish agency, action, control, intention, foreseeability, authority, or responsibility. Control is a scoped relationship in which a subject or mechanism has and may exercise effective pathways to select, initiate, continue, modify, inhibit, terminate, or materially constrain a target process or condition.**

These anchors are descriptive and analytical. They do not create a moral-agent class, a legal-person class, a universal autonomy threshold, or a responsible-party allocation. They also do not imply that every engineered control mechanism is an agent. The decisive boundary is not whether an occurrence was caused by a Human, artificial component, institution, or hybrid. It is what organized subject and level best explain the relevant selection and control, under what conditions and alternatives, with what causal model, and with what uncertainty.

The result therefore separates seven questions:

1. **Occurrence:** what event, process, output, behavior, action, omission, or consequence occurred?
2. **Causal Contribution:** what conditions or subjects made a relevant difference under the stated causal model?
3. **Agency:** what organized subject, if any, possessed and exercised the integrated capacity governing the relevant conduct?
4. **Control:** who or what had which effective intervention, selection, inhibition, or termination pathways over which target and period?
5. **Attribution and assessment:** who claims these relations, on what Evidence, under which alternatives, uncertainty, and competing explanations?
6. **Governance:** who had role, authority, authorization, duty, or standing, and which attribution or use is accepted?
7. **Responsibility and consequence:** who must answer, correct, repair, provide remedy, or bear another prospective or retrospective relation?

Stream A addresses the first five only far enough to prevent substitution. Streams B–E must address the governance and responsibility questions. A correct result, harmful outcome, valid authorization, contractual allocation, legal status, or later approval cannot retroactively manufacture or erase the underlying agency, action, causal, or control relations.

## 2. Accepted predecessor constraints

This analysis inherits and does not reopen the following:

1. Knowledge, Intelligence, capability, performance, origin, Participation, Contribution, Cooperation, Power, governance status, and successful outcome do not automatically entail agency, authority, responsibility, legal personality, or moral standing.
2. Actual bearing or occurrence, attribution claim, assessment, governance status, authority for use, Reliance Authorization, and record remain distinct.
3. Identity, Context, Scope, Domain, Relationship, Applicability, Bearer, Representation, Projection, and System retain their DR-010 meanings.
4. An Intelligence Bearer is an organized system under a scoped attribution. Intelligence does not entail agency; agency does not entail Intelligence.
5. Participant, Contributor, actor, resource, source, beneficiary, affected party, authority, represented subject, and responsible party remain distinct roles.
6. A causal background condition, interface inclusion, model invocation, tool use, organizational membership, legal registration, or system label does not establish Participation or agency.
7. Power is a scoped relational capacity to shape options, agenda, participation, interpretation, conduct, distribution, consequence, or revision. Power does not entail control, successful effect, authority, legitimacy, or responsibility.
8. Influence may shape conduct without establishing control. Constraint may limit action without establishing agency or justified authority.
9. Refusal, opposition, challenge, suppression, coercion, domination, and practical alternatives remain visible under DR-013.
10. Cooperative units, collectives, institutions, distributed arrangements, configured systems, and hybrids do not inherit member or component predicates automatically, nor do members or components inherit whole-system predicates.
11. Human, Scientific, and Engineering Views must remain aligned where material, with declared transformation, omission, and loss.
12. Detailed role, authority, authorization, delegation, duty, responsibility, accountability, answerability, remedy, liability, legal personality, moral standing, consent, and affected-party standing remain for Streams B–E.

## 3. Why SymK needs this boundary

The Product Vision requires SymK to record which Intelligence or institution contributed, decided, authorized, or remains responsible. Those verbs cannot share one undifferentiated subject field. Without a governed agency/action/control boundary, a project can:

- treat any model output as an action;
- treat any causal involvement as responsibility;
- call a designated approver the controller despite absent time, competence, information, intervention, or reversal capacity;
- call a provider uninvolved after contractually transferring the final decision while retaining model, update, interface, or information control;
- attribute agency to a legal entity because it can bear liability;
- deny artificial or hybrid agency solely because it lacks accepted personhood;
- assign all conduct to a configured system while hiding decisive member, component, institutional, or infrastructural Contributions;
- copy system-level agency to every member or component;
- infer authority or permission from capability;
- use the word “autonomous” as a product label rather than an evidenced, scoped comparison;
- make an audit log substitute for an answerable subject; or
- declare a responsibility gap where the actual failure is an incomplete causal, agency, or control representation.

The minimum boundary performs six jobs:

1. **Event description:** identify what happened without assuming an agent or norm.
2. **Causal analysis:** preserve multiple enabling, producing, preventing, amplifying, and constraining Contributions without flattening them.
3. **Subject attribution:** determine which organized subject and explanatory level, if any, governed the relevant conduct.
4. **Control visibility:** distinguish formal, nominal, potential, exercised, effective, partial, joint, distributed, and failed control.
5. **Non-substitution:** prevent action, agency, control, authority, authorization, responsibility, and outcome from creating one another.
6. **Downstream stability:** give Streams B–E stable inputs for role and responsibility without pre-deciding their normative conclusions.

SymK does not need a complete metaphysics of free will, philosophy of action, theory of causation, criminal-law doctrine, moral psychology, or universal control theory. It needs a bounded analytical minimum that works across Human, artificial, collective, institutional, distributed, and hybrid cases.

## 4. Minimum explanatory layers

| Layer | Working meaning | Does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | A state or circumstance relevant to possibility, constraint, interpretation, or consequence | Causal difference, action, agency, duty, or responsibility |
| Event | A temporally and contextually bounded occurrence or state transition | Agent, intention, action, cause, value, or authorization |
| Output | A produced signal, content, artifact, recommendation, score, command, or state | Action, decision, Knowledge, agency, authority, or responsibility |
| Behavior | An observed or inferred pattern of activity attributable to a subject, mechanism, component, or system | Integrated selection, agency, purpose, authorization, or moral standing |
| Execution or actuation | Performance of an instruction, operation, state transition, or control signal | Decision authority, agency at that level, responsibility, or authorship |
| Action | An event or process attributable to an exercise of agency for a declared target and Scope | Correctness, success, authorization, legitimacy, responsibility, or control of every consequence |
| Omission | Non-performance of a specified possible or expected action within a declared opportunity and time | Deliberate choice, causal Contribution, duty, fault, or responsibility |
| Causal Contribution | A difference made under an explicit causal model to an event, process, action, omission, condition, or consequence | Agency, control, intention, foreseeability, authority, blame, liability, or responsibility |
| Agency capacity | Scoped capacity of an organized subject to govern conduct through integrated selection and control responsive to relevant variation | Exercise, autonomy, Intelligence, personhood, authority, permission, or responsibility |
| Agency exercise | Manifest use of that capacity in the production, continuation, modification, inhibition, or termination of conduct | Complete control, successful outcome, authorization, or responsible qualification |
| Control capacity | Availability of effective pathways to select, intervene in, inhibit, terminate, or materially constrain a target | Exercise, success, agency of the controller, authority, legitimacy, or responsibility |
| Control exercise | Actual use of a control pathway against a stated target | Intended or successful effect, exclusive control, authority, or responsibility |
| Influence | Capacity or exercise that shapes probability, interpretation, preference, agenda, or conduct without the stronger intervention claim | Control, coercion, authority, responsibility, or outcome |
| Autonomy | Comparative degree to which an organized subject governs its conduct, action policies, or operative goals within a Scope without contemporaneous direction or decisive external control | Independence from all conditions, moral agency, authority, personhood, responsibility, or value |
| Decision | A selection or commitment that governs subsequent conduct within a declared process | Epistemic warrant, authority, authorization, execution, control, action, or responsibility |
| Consequence | A later state or effect connected to conduct under a stated model | Foreseeability, intention, agency relative to that effect, blame, liability, or value |

This is a dependency map, not a universal sequence. Some events contain several actions; one action may span many events. A subject may act while lacking control over a remote consequence. A mechanism may control a variable without qualifying as an agent. A decision may be made but not executed. An omission may causally matter without being deliberate. Several subjects may exercise different kinds of control over the same outcome.

## 5. Working agency model

### 5.1 Candidate minimum

An agency attribution requires all of the following to be addressed:

1. **Organized subject boundary:** identify the Human, organism, artificial system, team, institution, distributed arrangement, configured system, hybrid, or other organized subject proposed as agent.
2. **Declared conduct and Scope:** identify the conduct governed, not merely the final outcome or the subject's general capability.
3. **Materially differentiated conduct possibilities:** state what relevant alternatives, variations, continuation paths, inhibition states, or target changes existed.
4. **Integrated selection:** show how information, conditions, goals, policies, reasons, norms, dispositions, or other action-relevant inputs were integrated at the proposed subject level.
5. **Control connection:** show how the subject could initiate, continue, modify, inhibit, terminate, or materially constrain the conduct.
6. **Relevant-variation response:** show that materially different input, Context, feedback, challenge, or internal state would systematically produce or permit materially different conduct.
7. **Counterfactual and mechanistic support:** state what Evidence supports the attribution and what passive-transmission, external-control, coincidence, post hoc aggregation, or lower/higher-level explanations compete with it.
8. **Temporal qualification:** state when the capacity and exercise existed, including model, configuration, role, policy, and dependency versions where material.

“Integrated” means organized at the declared subject boundary. It does not mean uncaused, independent of substrate, free from environment, conscious, reflective, or morally responsible. “Alternatives” need not mean metaphysically undetermined possibilities. The minimum is a supportable difference in conduct under relevant variation or intervention, not a commitment to one theory of free will.

### 5.2 Actual agency, attribution, and status

Agency must be represented at separate levels:

| Level | Question | Prohibited substitution |
|---|---|---|
| Actual agency capacity | Does the organized subject possess the scoped capacity? | A label, benchmark, role, legal status, or successful output creates it |
| Actual agency exercise | Was that capacity exercised in the relevant conduct? | General capacity proves this exercise |
| Agency attribution | Who claims agency of which subject for what conduct, Scope, and period? | The claim proves its content |
| Agency assessment | What Evidence, counterevidence, criteria, causal model, and competing boundaries support or defeat the attribution? | A score, explanation, or audit creates agency |
| Governance status | Is the attribution accepted, challenged, restricted, or rejected for a stated use? | Accepted status creates actual agency or moral agency |
| Agency record | What Representation preserves the attribution, assessment, status, and lineage? | A runtime identity, account, role row, or record is the agent |

### 5.3 Operational agency is not moral agency

The working candidate is intentionally substrate-neutral and normatively thin. A configured artificial system, institution, or hybrid may satisfy operational agency conditions in a Scope. That finding would not establish consciousness, sentience, personhood, moral standing, rights, duties, legal personality, blameworthiness, liability, authority, or authorization.

Conversely, a Human, legal person, official, or institution does not satisfy a particular agency attribution merely through type or status. A Human may be unconscious, deceived, coerced, deprived of practical alternatives, or limited to a ceremonial click. A corporation may bear legal liability while the conduct at issue is better explained through member, process, or distributed agency. Legal and moral classifications may refer to agency evidence, but they do not create the descriptive relation.

### 5.4 Agency is conduct-relative

A subject may be an agent with respect to selecting and sending a message but not with respect to the model-generated content it did not inspect, understand, or control. A configured artificial system may be an agent with respect to resource allocation but not with respect to an upstream objective fixed by another subject. An institution may act through a governed decision process while no individual controls the complete institutional outcome.

Agency claims must therefore name the target conduct. “The Human acted,” “the AI decided,” or “the organization chose” is incomplete without the subject boundary, conduct, mechanism, alternatives, and level.

## 6. Action, execution, behavior, and output boundary

### 6.1 Action

An action is not every state change produced through a subject. It is a state change or process attributable to an agency exercise. The attribution should identify:

- the proposed agent and its boundary;
- the target conduct and period;
- the integrated selection or control process;
- the action pathway or mechanism;
- relevant alternatives and constraints;
- direct and mediated execution;
- the distinction between intended or selected conduct and downstream consequence; and
- Evidence, uncertainty, and competing explanations.

Action may be direct or mediated. A Human can act through a tool; an institution can act through an authorized process; a distributed system can act through coordinated nodes; a hybrid can act through integrated Human and artificial contributions. Mediation does not erase agency, but neither does tool invocation automatically make every output the user's action.

### 6.2 Execution and actuation

Execution is performance of an instruction or operation. It may be:

- an exercise of the executor's own agency;
- a mechanically realized part of another agent's action;
- joint or nested action at several levels; or
- behavior not yet shown to be action.

A model call, API invocation, robotic movement, database write, signed document, or button click establishes an event and perhaps execution. It does not settle who selected the operative alternatives, who controlled the process, whether a decision occurred, or who is responsible.

### 6.3 Behavior and output

Behavior is broader than action. It can describe patterns before agency is established and can apply to components or mechanisms. Output is broader still: it identifies a produced result without deciding whether it is behavior, action, decision, Knowledge, or answer.

Using this wider vocabulary is not a denial of artificial or collective agency. It is a way to prevent agency from being smuggled into the description before the subject and control tests are passed.

### 6.4 Action is not consequence

An agent may control an action without controlling all effects. A Human may intentionally deploy a system without intending a discriminatory outcome. A model may select an output without controlling how a deployer uses it. An institution may authorize a policy without controlling every local implementation. Agency relative to the initial conduct cannot be copied to every consequence, and lack of control over a remote effect cannot erase the initial action.

The causal chain, foreseeability, role, authority, duty, and responsibility require later analysis.

## 7. Omission boundary

An omission claim must identify:

1. the action not performed;
2. the subject proposed as able or expected to act;
3. the relevant opportunity and time window;
4. practical availability, information, access, capability, and control;
5. the baseline or expectation against which non-performance is described;
6. the causal model connecting non-performance to a consequence;
7. whether non-performance was selected, habitual, forgotten, unnoticed, coerced, impossible, or produced by another constraint; and
8. the source of any duty or expectation, if later asserted.

Omission, agentic omission, culpable omission, and responsible omission are not synonyms.

- A sensor may fail to signal: this is a missing event or system failure before agency is shown.
- A Human may not intervene because the interface hid the relevant state: non-intervention occurred, but practical control may be absent.
- An operator may deliberately leave an available stop control unused: agentic non-performance may be supportable, while duty and responsibility remain later.
- An institution may systematically fail to create an escalation path: the omission may exist at an institutional or process level even if no single member selected the full absence.

No universal duty is inferred from causal relevance or capability alone.

## 8. Causal Contribution boundary

### 8.1 Model-relative attribution

A causal Contribution claim must state:

- the outcome, event, action, omission, or condition being explained;
- the subject, mechanism, condition, Representation, policy, data, resource, or event proposed as contributor;
- the causal model and level;
- the comparison or counterfactual baseline;
- producing, enabling, constraining, amplifying, preventing, maintaining, or inhibiting role where material;
- temporal order and relevant dependencies;
- uncertainty and competing explanations; and
- whether the claim concerns necessity, sufficiency, probability change, mechanism, intervention, or another causal relation.

SymK does not select one universal causal formalism in 2.1.6. Counterfactual, mechanistic, statistical, process, and Domain-specific causal accounts may contribute within their authority. Their differences and limitations must remain explicit.

### 8.2 Causal Contribution is broader than agency

Training data, interface defaults, incentives, resource constraints, a storm, a hardware fault, an unavailable alternative, a Human instruction, a model output, a deployment choice, and a downstream misuse can all be causally relevant. Some are conditions, mechanisms, actions, or omissions; not all are agents or exercises of control.

### 8.3 Causal Contribution is not responsibility

The following substitutions are prohibited:

- necessary cause therefore sole responsible party;
- final or proximate act therefore exhaustive responsibility;
- causal remoteness therefore no responsibility;
- contractual disclaimer therefore no causal Contribution;
- legal liability therefore factual cause;
- intention therefore causation;
- foreseeability therefore control;
- control therefore causation of every effect;
- system-level cause therefore every member caused the effect; or
- many contributors therefore nobody can answer.

Streams C and D must determine how causal, role, prospective, answerability, blame, liability, and remedial relations interact without copying one into another.

## 9. Control model

### 9.1 Control is a typed relationship

A control claim is incomplete unless it states:

1. **controller candidate:** the subject or mechanism proposed as holding or exercising control;
2. **target:** the action, process, component, resource, option set, information flow, objective, policy, deployment, environment, or consequence being controlled;
3. **Scope and time:** where and when the pathway exists;
4. **mechanism:** how observation, selection, intervention, inhibition, termination, modification, or constraint occurs;
5. **information condition:** what state the controller can detect or infer in time to act;
6. **practical alternatives:** which interventions are materially available rather than merely documented;
7. **dependencies and constraints:** what other subjects, infrastructure, permissions, skills, time, incentives, or risks condition the pathway;
8. **exercise:** whether and how the pathway was actually used;
9. **effect:** whether the attempted control changed the target as intended; and
10. **Evidence and status:** what supports the claim and whether it remains contested.

Control is not one scalar. Depending on the case, material dimensions may include observability, interpretability, option selection, initiation, continuation, modification, inhibition, veto, stop, rollback, repair, objective setting, agenda setting, resource allocation, access, configuration, update, deployment, monitoring, and decommissioning. This list is analytical, not a universal schema or metric.

### 9.2 Capacity, exercise, and effect

- **Control capacity** can exist without exercise.
- **Control exercise** can occur without the intended effect.
- **Control effect** can occur without exclusive control or complete understanding.
- **Successful outcome** can occur without control by the subject credited.
- **Failed outcome** does not prove absence of control.

The attribution must not be inferred from outcome alone.

### 9.3 Nominal and effective control

Nominal control is a declared role, permission, button, approval field, contractual clause, or supposed oversight relation. Effective control requires a usable pathway under real conditions. Relevant questions include whether the subject had:

- timely access to the target state;
- enough intelligibility to identify a material issue;
- competence or support appropriate to the intervention;
- time and attention;
- practical permission and protection;
- a feasible alternative;
- authority where authority is required for the mechanism to operate;
- an intervention whose effect was not already foreclosed; and
- a correction, stop, rollback, or escalation route proportionate to consequence.

A Human click can be a real action and a formally authorized approval while providing no meaningful review or outcome control. The same Human may control whether a workflow advances while not controlling the content, evidence, model, objective, or downstream consequence. Control must be target-specific.

### 9.4 Joint, distributed, and asymmetric control

Several subjects may control different targets or dimensions:

- a provider controls model design and updates;
- a deployer controls Context, population, integration, and operational threshold;
- an operator controls initiation or local override;
- an institution controls policy, incentives, staffing, and escalation;
- a user controls a request or misuse;
- an artificial component controls low-level selection or actuation;
- infrastructure controls availability or permitted pathways; and
- affected parties may have no control despite bearing consequences.

Control can therefore be joint, nested, sequential, distributed, overlapping, or asymmetric. No subject becomes the sole controller merely by occupying the last step. Streams C and D must later test responsibility allocation against this typed control graph.

## 10. Agency, control, Power, autonomy, and authority

| Relation | Stream A boundary |
|---|---|
| Agency without broad control | A constrained subject may govern its own local conduct while another controls objectives, resources, options, or consequences |
| Control without agency | An engineered regulator or access mechanism may control a target in the engineering sense before an organized subject-level agency attribution is justified |
| Power without exercised control | A subject may possess a durable capacity to shape options or agenda without using it in the case |
| Influence without control | Advice, defaults, framing, prestige, or prediction may shape conduct without an effective intervention pathway |
| Autonomy without authority | A system may operate with limited contemporaneous direction while lacking permission or recognized standing |
| Authority without effective control | An official may possess recognized standing but lack information, access, competence, time, or practical intervention capacity |
| Authorization without agency | A permission can be assigned to a passive account, nominal role, or subject unable to act |
| Agency without authorization | A subject can act outside, before, or against permission; emergency and misuse cases make this visible |

Autonomy is comparative and scoped. It must state autonomy from which contemporaneous direction or external control, over which conduct and operative goals, under which dependencies and constraints. Automation, persistence, unsupervised runtime, self-description, adaptation, or output novelty is insufficient by itself. No autonomy classification is accepted in Stream A.

Authority and authorization belong substantively to Stream B. Here they are preserved only to prohibit inference from agency or control.

## 11. Subject and level tests

| Candidate subject | Possible Stream A finding | Boundary requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Individual Human | Agency and action may be evidenced for specified conduct | Human origin, signature, approval, job title, or physical proximity is insufficient |
| Artificial model | Often a capability-bearing component, source, or resource rather than the complete runtime agent | Identify whether selection and control occur in the model, wrapper, runtime, configured service, institution, or hybrid |
| Configured artificial system | May satisfy operational agency for bounded conduct | Preserve model, policy, tool, memory, environment, operator, and infrastructure dependencies and versions |
| Team or collective | May support collective agency for integrated conduct while members also act at their levels | Show organization, selection, integration, control pathway, boundary, and counterfactual difference; do not infer from plurality or consensus |
| Institution | May act through persistent governed processes despite member turnover | Legal personality, name, charter, or liability does not prove actual agency for the conduct; process and control evidence remain necessary |
| Distributed arrangement | May support distributed agency when no one node explains integrated selection and control | Network connection or aggregate output is insufficient; coordination, integration, failure boundaries, and dependency must be explicit |
| Hybrid Human–artificial system | May support hybrid agency where the integrated whole explains conduct better than either component alone | Preserve component actions, information asymmetries, vetoes, dependencies, timing, and alternative Human-only or artificial-only explanations |
| Component or infrastructure | May execute, actuate, constrain, or causally contribute without subject-level agency | Do not upgrade technical control or causal centrality automatically to agency |
| Legal entity | May or may not coincide with an institution-level agent | Legal recognition, ownership, liability, and agency are separately evidenced |
| Affected nonparticipant | Need not act or control anything to remain inside the consequence horizon | Lack of agency, Participation, contract, or account cannot erase effect or later standing analysis |

### 11.1 Lowest-adequate and whole-required explanation

Prefer the lowest organized subject level that explains the conduct without omitting constitutive integration. Move to a collective, institutional, distributed, configured-system, or hybrid level only when member or component accounts cannot explain the integrated selection and control. Retain lower-level Contributions after moving upward.

This is not a rule that the lowest level is always causally or normatively primary. It prevents convenient whole-system reification while permitting genuine higher-level agency when organization does explanatory work.

### 11.2 No property transfer

- Member agency does not automatically create collective agency.
- Collective agency does not make every member an agent of every collective action.
- Component control does not automatically create system agency.
- System agency does not give each component agency.
- Hybrid agency does not erase Human or artificial Contributions.
- Institutional agency does not settle member, officer, contractor, or successor responsibility.
- Agency at one time or version does not persist automatically after update, replacement, merger, fork, or dissolution.

## 12. Candidate approaches considered

### A — Agency restricted to Humans or natural persons

**Rejected as a universal SymK boundary.** It gives origin categorical privilege, conflicts with the non-anthropocentric Intelligence scope, cannot represent institutional or hybrid action honestly, and confuses operational agency with moral or legal personhood.

### B — Intelligence, capability, planning, or learning entails agency

**Rejected.** Intelligence and capability may exist without action control; planning may be an output; Learning may change a system without giving it agency in the relevant conduct. Separate Evidence is required.

### C — Every cause or behavior is an action by an agent

**Rejected.** It turns conditions, components, faults, natural events, outputs, and passive transmission into agents and makes responsibility laundering easier.

### D — Only conscious, intentional, reasons-responsive, or morally answerable subjects have agency

**Rejected as the minimum operational boundary and preserved as a possible stronger specialization.** It imports disputed mental and moral conditions, excludes routine, habitual, institutional, artificial, and constrained conduct prematurely, and makes agency depend on concepts assigned later or outside SymK jurisdiction.

### E — Role, authorization, account identity, or legal personality establishes agency

**Rejected.** Governance and legal classifications may allocate permissions or consequences without proving the actual selection and control relation.

### F — Agency as integrated, variation-responsive selection and control by a scoped organized subject

**Retained provisionally.** It is substrate-neutral without being boundary-free, supports Human, artificial, collective, institutional, distributed, and hybrid testing, distinguishes execution and technical control from agency, and leaves moral, legal, authority, and responsibility questions open.

## 14. Three-view preservation obligations

### 14.1 Human View

The Human View should preserve:

- what subjects understood themselves and others to be doing;
- intended conduct, perceived alternatives, purposes, reasons, instructions, expectations, uncertainty, and constraints where material;
- practical rather than merely formal control, including time, competence, information, fear, dependency, refusal, and intervention experience;
- disagreement over the agent or system boundary;
- affected-party experience and consequences; and
- why an action, agency, autonomy, causal, or control attribution is accepted or challenged.

Human testimony and explanation are Evidence, not infallible semantic authority.

### 14.2 Scientific View

The Scientific View should preserve, where material:

- explicit causal questions, models, baselines, variables, mechanisms, interventions, and competing explanations;
- observed and inferred behavior under relevant variation;
- evidence for selection, integration, control, and subject boundary;
- counterfactual, experimental, statistical, mechanistic, process, or comparative support and their limitations;
- uncertainty, confounding, robustness, failed predictions, and conditions of defeat; and
- alternative individual, component, collective, institutional, distributed, or hybrid explanations.

No behavioral metric, benchmark, causal coefficient, model explanation, or prediction accuracy defines agency or control universally.

### 14.3 Engineering View

The Engineering View should eventually preserve:

- runtime subjects, models, versions, configurations, components, interfaces, tools, policies, resources, environments, and dependencies;
- input, state, output, event, execution, intervention, inhibition, stop, rollback, update, and consequence paths;
- Human and automated observation, decision, approval, authorization, execution, escalation, and override points as separate fields or relations where material;
- nominal permissions versus tested effective pathways;
- timing, latency, access, failure, uncertainty, and control loss;
- causal and agency attribution claims separately from logs and workflow statuses; and
- transformations, omissions, aggregation, and declared Projection loss.

This stream selects no event model, causal language, action ontology, agent architecture, control calculus, schema, workflow, access-control system, audit format, or runtime identity design.

### 14.4 Alignment rule

A narrative saying “a Human remained in control,” a study showing average intervention success, and an interface containing an approval button do not establish aligned control when runtime timing, information, competence, incentives, or rollback make intervention ineffective. Likewise, an Engineering trace showing automated selection cannot settle moral or legal agency. Material mismatch keeps the claim challenged and must not be hidden by aggregation.

## 16. Consequences for governing artifacts

### Product Vision

The Product Vision's “contributed, decided, authorized, or remains responsible” requirement needs separate subject and relation claims. A DIA must not report one generic owner, actor, approver, or agent field. “Responsible for application and consequences” remains a normative requirement to be analyzed later; Stream A supplies the factual agency, causal, action, and control inputs it cannot replace.

### Candidate A0

A0's responsible-Cooperation direction requires agency and control visibility but cannot infer them from Participation or Cooperation. A cooperative episode may contain actions by several agents, execution by non-agent mechanisms, affected nonparticipants, and control outside the participant boundary. This is an analytical consequence for 2.2, not an A0 revision.

### Candidate A1

A1's authority/warrant separation is reinforced. Approval and authorization may govern action while failing to create Knowledge. Stream A adds that approval may itself be an action without meaningful control of the approved content or consequence.

### Candidate A2

A2 is directionally compatible but its current compact definitions cannot govern 2.1.6 by inheritance. In particular:

- capability, role, agency, authority, authorization, control, responsibility, and standing must remain separate;
- Human approval must be tested for effective review and target-specific control;
- revisability does not itself preserve practical intervention or responsibility continuity;
- role records cannot create the represented relations; and
- artificial, collective, institutional, distributed, and hybrid agency cannot be accepted or rejected solely by origin or legal status.

The A2 engineering consequence should eventually distinguish nominal from effective control and actual causal/agency relations from their records. Selection and wording remain for 2.2.

### Candidate A4

Engineering epistemic conditions can causally shape what becomes knowable, relied upon, or acted upon without directly controlling every result. Design, update, interface, data, model, validation, deployment, monitoring, and decommissioning control must remain visible. Technical mediation is not neutral, but causal relevance alone does not allocate responsibility.

### Candidate A6 and J1

A6 must later preserve role and control changes across inheritance, exception, update, replacement, and migration. J1 must preserve Domain law, professional duty, institutional authority, and causal expertise without allowing any one jurisdictional account to become universal SymK semantics.

### Foundation Papers

- The Intelligence paper must separate capability, agency, autonomy, action, control, personhood, authority, and responsibility and avoid both Human exclusivity and artificial moral-status inference.
- The Knowledge paper must distinguish source, causal Contribution, epistemic Contribution, action, decision, authority, approval, Reliance Authorization, and responsibility.

## 20. Confidence and reopening conditions

**Confidence: High** that event, output, behavior, execution, action, omission, causal Contribution, agency, control, authority, authorization, responsibility, and consequence must remain distinguishable; that causal Contribution is neither agency nor responsibility; that control is target-, Scope-, mechanism-, time-, and alternative-specific; that nominal Human approval is not meaningful control; and that legal or moral status cannot create descriptive agency.

**Confidence: Medium** in the exact operational agency anchor, especially integrated selection, relevant variation, and the relation between agency and engineered control mechanisms. The working model is strong enough for Stream B but remains deliberately revisable.

**Confidence: Low to Medium** on the minimum threshold for artificial, simple-controller, collective, institutional, distributed, and hybrid agency; the action status of omission; and the exact autonomy boundary. These require later streams, Domain counterevidence, and 2.1.7 integration.

The working model must be reopened if later analysis shows that:

- the operational agency minimum admits passive mechanisms indiscriminately or excludes clear Human, institutional, artificial, or hybrid action;
- agency cannot be separated from authority or responsibility without making the concept empty;
- action cannot be distinguished from execution or behavior across relevant project cases;
- the causal model either erases structural Contributions or expands without a usable relevance boundary;
- nominal/effective control cannot be distinguished without importing one Domain procedure;
- multi-level agency cannot preserve both whole-system integration and member/component Contributions;
- omission analysis requires duty to identify the event at all rather than only to qualify it;
- three-view alignment imposes disproportionate representation burdens or permits responsibility laundering;
- derived-project grounding produces systematic false positives or false negatives;
- or the model contradicts the Product Vision or Stage-Accepted DR-009 through DR-013.
