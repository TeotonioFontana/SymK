## 1. Question and analytical result

> How must SymK represent and test responsibility allocation when causal Contributions, roles, authority, control, duties, decisions, supervision, legal status, answerability, and remedy capacity are distributed across individuals, components, collectives, institutions, organizations, external authorities, and Human–artificial hybrids?

Stream D supports the following working answer:

> **A responsibility allocation claim is a governed mapping that connects one or more specified responsibility relations to one or more identified subjects at declared levels for stated objects, sources, governing frames, Domain, Context, Scope, phase, time, and conditions. It must preserve the basis and Evidence for each relation; what is distinct, shared, joint, overlapping, transferred, retained, inherited, disputed, or unallocated; the capability, authority, control, answerability, and response paths needed for performance; affected subjects and external authority; and status, challenge, review, succession, and record. An allocation record does not create a valid responsibility relation by itself.**

> **Distributed responsibility means that material responsibility relations are spread across multiple subjects, levels, locations, phases, or governing sources, possibly without one subject holding complete agency, control, information, authority, or causal sufficiency. Shared responsibility means that multiple subjects bear responsibility relations concerning the same object; joint responsibility means that an applicable source constitutes a coordinated relation whose fulfillment or assessment depends materially on more than one subject. Distinct, shared, joint, collective, institutional, distributed, and hybrid relations may coexist. They are not synonyms, do not imply equality, and need not form additive shares.**

> **Collective or institutional responsibility is a whole-level relation supported under a declared frame by the relevant constitution, identity, organization, process, authority, decision, control, role, continuity, capacity, answerability, or legal status Evidence. It is not the sum of member responsibility and does not copy to every member. Member or component relations do not automatically aggregate into a whole-level relation. A representative can answer for a whole without becoming the sole causal or responsible subject.**

> **Hybrid allocation preserves Human, artificial, organizational, infrastructural, and configured-whole relations separately. Artificial action, control, recommendation, decision, execution, monitoring, explanation, or record does not make an artificial component the universal responsibility sink; Human status or final approval does not make a Human the universal residual bearer. Every purported transfer through automation, delegation, outsourcing, procurement, contract, update, maintenance, replacement, merger, dissolution, or succession must state exactly what moves and what remains.**

> **A responsibility gap is not established merely because conduct is complex, distributed, opaque, automated, jointly caused, or not attributable to one blameworthy individual. A material gap exists only relative to a stated responsibility object and governing need when no valid, capable, reachable, and sufficiently empowered bearer or forum can perform, answer, assess, correct, or route the relation claimed to be required. The gap may be semantic, evidentiary, attributional, allocative, authoritative, capability-based, enforcement-related, remedial, temporal, or conflict-based; it may be genuine, manufactured, representational, or a governance failure.**

The working model therefore rejects a fixed “responsibility pie.” Responsibilities are typed relations, not a conserved substance. One subject's relation does not automatically reduce or eliminate another's; several subjects can bear different or overlapping responsibilities for the same object; and a share or percentage is meaningful only when a declared governing frame defines what is being measured and why additivity is justified.

The result separates eleven questions:

1. **Object:** what role, condition, risk, decision, action, omission, artifact, process, consequence, answer, correction, or remedy is being allocated?
2. **Relation kind:** is the claim about prospective responsibility, duty, answerability, accountability, blame, liability, remedy, correction, supervision, or another declared relation?
3. **Subject and level:** which individual, component, team, office, collective, institution, legal entity, network, configured system, hybrid, predecessor, successor, representative, or external authority is named?
4. **Connection:** what causal, agency, control, role, authority, authorization, decision, delegation, supervision, reliance, dependence, benefit, or consequence Evidence supports the claim?
5. **Source and frame:** under which authority, jurisdiction, standard, undertaking, contract, professional rule, organization, project, external law, or other frame is the allocation asserted?
6. **Allocation form:** is the relation distinct, parallel, sequential, nested, shared, joint, overlapping, retained, residual, transferred, inherited, vicarious, collective, institutional, distributed, hybrid, or presently unallocated?
7. **Performance conditions:** does each proposed bearer have the required information, capability, resources, authority, authorization, control, time, independence, and practical alternatives?
8. **Response architecture:** who can call for an account, assess, challenge, correct, contain, repair, remedy, review, appeal, or route the issue?
9. **Temporal boundary:** what applies before and after delegation, deployment, update, maintenance, replacement, merger, dissolution, succession, or decommissioning?
10. **Gap status:** what is missing, why, whether it is genuine or manufactured, who had authority or capacity to prevent it, and what remains unresolved?
11. **Governance state:** what relation validly holds under the declared frame, and what has merely been assigned, attributed, assessed, accepted, performed, discharged, breached, enforced, remedied, or recorded?

Stream D analyzes allocation architecture without assigning any actual responsibility. Stream E must determine the minimum ethical boundary, consent, affected-party standing, challenge, and repair. Stream F must consolidate the entire 2.1.6 system before any Proposed DR-014 may be prepared.

## 3. Why SymK needs an allocation model

SymK's value chain is socio-technical and epistemic. Data, representations, models, tools, methods, institutions, professional authority, deployment choices, users, affected populations, and feedback can all shape answers and consequences. A single “responsible owner” field cannot preserve this structure.

Without a governed allocation model:

- responsibility is copied to the last actor, final approver, contractual owner, nearest Human, artificial component, or legal entity;
- distributed causation is mistaken for equal responsibility or no responsibility;
- a whole is reified from member activity, or institutional responsibility is dissolved into isolated individuals;
- provider, developer, integrator, deployer, operator, user, maintainer, auditor, and authority functions collapse into job titles;
- a data artifact is treated as a responsible source, while the subjects governing collection, selection, maintenance, and use disappear;
- outsourcing creates distance that is mistaken for transfer;
- procurement contracts overwrite retained design, information, update, deployment, supervision, and external duties;
- an artificial component becomes the blame sink for conduct configured, selected, authorized, deployed, or maintained elsewhere;
- Human approval becomes the residual sink even when review is ceremonial;
- compliance, certification, audit, insurance, or legal personality is taken to exhaust every responsibility kind;
- percentages create false precision across incomparable duties, causal links, liabilities, and remedies;
- organizations expand their boundary to claim success and shrink it to deny harm;
- component updates or organizational succession erase historical relations;
- affected subjects fall outside the allocation because they hold no operational role;
- responsibility gaps are declared inevitable instead of diagnosed; or
- detailed graphs create accountability theater while no bearer can answer, correct, repair, or provide remedy.

The minimum model performs nine jobs:

1. **Object decomposition:** allocate explicit responsibility objects rather than the undifferentiated word “responsibility.”
2. **Level preservation:** keep individual, component, role, team, whole, institution, network, configured-system, hybrid, legal, predecessor, successor, representative, and affected-subject levels distinct.
3. **Relation typing:** preserve prospective, retrospective, duty, answerability, accountability, blame, liability, remedy, correction, and other relation kinds.
4. **Connection tracing:** retain causal, agency, control, role, authority, decision, delegation, supervision, reliance, benefit, and consequence Evidence without treating any as universally sufficient.
5. **Overlap integrity:** represent distinct, shared, joint, overlapping, transferred, retained, inherited, and unresolved relations without an assumed conservation law.
6. **Boundary invariance:** prevent subject, system, causal, temporal, or organizational boundaries from changing opportunistically with outcome or desired attribution.
7. **Lifecycle continuity:** expose allocation changes across design, procurement, integration, deployment, operation, update, maintenance, incident, repair, replacement, merger, dissolution, succession, and retirement.
8. **Gap diagnosis:** distinguish genuine absence from manufactured, representational, evidentiary, allocative, authority, capability, enforcement, remedy, continuity, or conflict defects.
9. **Response coverage:** connect allocation to practical answerability, assessment, correction, remedy, review, and affected-party horizons without preselecting ethical or legal rules.

SymK does not need to define one corporate model, supply-chain law, professional division of labor, joint-liability doctrine, governance board, accountability metric, risk score, insurance arrangement, or responsibility-assignment matrix. It needs universal non-substitution and preservation rules so those systems can be represented without laundering or inventing responsibility.

## 4. Material responsibility allocation claim

### 4.1 Required dimensions

A material allocation claim should identify:

1. allocation identifier, asserting source, and authority;
2. responsibility object and phase;
3. relation kind or kinds being allocated;
4. each proposed bearer and exact subject level;
5. Domain, Context, Scope, population, stakes, time, and version;
6. governing source, frame, jurisdiction, standard, and priority claim;
7. causal, agency, control, role, authority, authorization, decision, delegation, supervision, reliance, dependence, benefit, and consequence connections;
8. Evidence, uncertainty, disagreement, and alternative explanations for each material connection;
9. allocation form: distinct, sequential, parallel, nested, shared, joint, overlapping, retained, residual, transferred, inherited, vicarious, collective, institutional, distributed, hybrid, or unresolved;
10. information, capability, resources, authority, authorization, control, independence, time, and practical alternatives required for performance;
11. delegator, delegate, contractor, principal, representative, provider, recipient, predecessor, successor, and retained relations where applicable;
12. affected subjects, claimants, beneficiaries, represented subjects, and protected interests;
13. answerability, forum, assessment, challenge, correction, containment, remedy, enforcement, review, appeal, and routing paths;
14. performance, discharge, breach, conflict, impossibility, expiry, replacement, succession, and reopening conditions;
15. current status and who assigned or assessed it; and
16. record, Provenance, historical state, and Projection loss.

This list is an analytical preservation obligation, not an accepted schema. A Domain can use another representation if it preserves the same material distinctions and declares losses.

### 4.2 Allocation is not relation creation

An allocation artifact can:

- propose a responsibility relation;
- assign a role or duty under an authority;
- record an existing relation;
- allocate operational work;
- distribute answerability or review;
- route a claim or remedy;
- express an external legal or contractual allocation; or
- document an assessment.

It does not prove that the source is valid, the relation applies, the bearer can perform, external authority recognizes the transfer, affected parties are represented, the allocation is legitimate, the response path is effective, or the record is accurate. These questions remain independently challengeable.

### 4.3 Object-first allocation

Allocation begins with objects, not names. “Provider is responsible” is incomplete. A useful claim may instead distinguish responsibility for:

- training-data collection, rights, quality, Provenance, or maintenance;
- model design, evaluation, limits, updates, or disclosures;
- integration assumptions and interface behavior;
- purpose, procurement, Domain fit, deployment Context, population, thresholds, or reliance;
- decision, approval, authorization, execution, or Human review;
- monitoring, incident detection, containment, rollback, correction, maintenance, or decommissioning;
- user conduct, misuse, configuration, or local data;
- institutional policy, resources, incentives, supervision, challenge, or answerability;
- current or historical records;
- affected-party communication, remedy, repair, or prevention; and
- external compliance, professional judgment, adjudication, or enforcement.

Different objects can have different bearers, sources, phases, and response paths. A single label cannot substitute for the map.

## 5. Subject and level architecture

### 5.1 Level set

Stream D tests at least:

- individual Human;
- individual artificial component, model, agent, service, or tool;
- role, office, account, or position distinct from its occupant;
- Human team, committee, panel, profession, or community;
- artificial multi-component arrangement;
- organization, institution, public body, or legal entity;
- consortium, supply chain, platform ecosystem, market, network, or distributed process;
- configured operational system;
- Human–artificial hybrid;
- provider, customer, contractor, principal, representative, predecessor, successor, maintainer, custodian, or insurer relation;
- external authority, regulator, court, professional body, auditor, or reviewer; and
- affected, represented, claimant, beneficiary, or nonparticipant subject.

This list is non-exhaustive and contains overlapping kinds. It creates no bearer eligibility or responsibility by itself.

### 5.2 Level-specific Evidence

A claim at one level needs Evidence for that level:

- **individual:** conduct, knowledge, alternatives, role, authority, control, duty, and response capacity of the identified individual;
- **component:** actual function, configuration, information, decision or control pathway, version, dependency, and operating Context;
- **role or office:** source, functions, authority, continuity, occupancy, resources, and performance conditions;
- **team or committee:** membership, procedure, agenda, information, deliberation, decision rule, dissent, authority, and collective action;
- **institution or organization:** constituted identity, policy, authority, resources, incentives, process integration, decision/control pathways, continuity, representation, and answerability;
- **legal entity:** applicable legal status and jurisdiction, distinct from operational integration;
- **distributed arrangement:** nodes, connections, protocols, dependencies, coordination, boundary, governance, emergent effects, and absence or presence of central control;
- **configured system:** exact components, versions, settings, operators, data, infrastructure, interfaces, policies, and environment;
- **hybrid:** Human and artificial contributions, dependencies, decision points, authority, control, interface effects, feedback, and whole-level organization; and
- **successor:** continuity source, transferred relations, records, capacity, authority, and affected-party path.

Evidence for a member cannot simply be copied to the collective. Evidence for a whole cannot simply be copied to every member.

### 5.3 Representative and represented subject

A representative may:

- provide an account for a whole;
- receive a claim or notice;
- make an authorized decision;
- perform a remedy or corrective act;
- preserve continuity across member change; or
- communicate an institutional position.

Representation does not make the representative the sole agent, decision-maker, causal contributor, liable subject, blameworthy party, or owner of every remedy. The represented subject, authority, Scope, knowledge access, constraints, conflicts, and response power remain explicit.

### 5.4 Affected subjects remain outside the allocation sink

Affected subjects may have no operational responsibility, agency, role, contract, account, or causal Contribution. They remain necessary to:

- define the consequence horizon;
- test whether responsibilities were scoped adequately;
- expose dependency, vulnerability, Power, and practical alternatives;
- identify challenge, representation, correction, and remedy gaps; and
- prevent allocation from being evaluated only among system operators.

Stream D preserves this horizon. Stream E decides the minimum ethical force, standing, consent, and remedy requirements.

The same subject can occupy several functions; several subjects can occupy one function; an artificial component can perform operational functions without acquiring every related responsibility kind; and titles differ across Domains. Function and occupant must remain separate.

### 6.2 Data source is not automatically a responsible subject

“Data source” may mean:

- an originating Human or institution;
- an affected or represented subject;
- a collector or sensor operator;
- a publisher, repository, database, or artifact;
- a curator, licensor, purchaser, or data steward;
- a transformation or synthetic-generation process; or
- a technical endpoint.

Artifacts, datasets, repositories, and endpoints are not responsible bearers merely because they are called sources. The subjects governing collection, representation, selection, transformation, access, licensing, maintenance, and use remain distinct. A represented or affected subject is not made responsible for downstream use merely because data concerns them.

### 6.3 Audit and assurance are scoped

An auditor or assessor is not universally responsible for the audited system. A material allocation must preserve:

- assessment object and period;
- criteria and authority;
- Evidence access and sampling;
- independence and conflicts;
- exclusions, uncertainty, and declared loss;
- findings, recommendations, and decision status;
- correction or escalation authority; and
- reliance authorized by whom and for what Scope.

Certification or audit can create reliance and answerability relations under a frame without transferring every design, deployment, operation, or remedy responsibility.

## 7. Allocation forms and non-additivity

### 7.1 Distinct allocation

Different subjects bear different relation kinds or objects. For example, a provider may answer for disclosed model limits, a deployer for population selection, an operator for an intentional misuse, and an institution for remedy access. Distinct does not mean isolated; the relations can depend on each other.

### 7.2 Sequential allocation

Responsibility objects move through lifecycle phases. Source governance, design, integration, authorization, deployment, operation, maintenance, and remedy may have different bearers. A later phase does not erase an earlier relation, and an earlier contribution does not imply responsibility for every later use.

### 7.3 Parallel allocation

Several subjects simultaneously govern different aspects of one object: semantic validity, technical safety, professional appropriateness, legal compliance, operational control, affected-party communication, and remedy. Parallel claims may conflict and need routing.

### 7.4 Nested allocation

An individual may bear a role duty within a team; the team may have a coordinated function within an institution; the institution may bear legal or public responsibility; a project may inherit SymK commitments. Each level requires its own source and Evidence.

### 7.5 Shared responsibility

Several subjects bear responsibility relations concerning the same object. The relations may be:

- the same type under one source;
- different types under one or several sources;
- overlapping in Scope;
- conditional on another subject's conduct;
- individually sufficient, jointly necessary, or mutually dependent; or
- non-comparable.

“Shared” does not mean equal, diluted, collective, joint, or additive.

### 7.6 Joint responsibility

Joint responsibility exists only when a governing source constitutes a coordinated relation whose fulfillment, breach, assessment, or response materially depends on more than one subject. It should identify the joint object, members or parties, source, decision and performance rule, internal allocation, external answerability, exit, replacement, and failure conditions.

Working together, co-signing, or belonging to one organization does not establish joint responsibility automatically.

### 7.7 Collective and institutional allocation

A whole-level relation can coexist with member-level relations. It may be supported by:

- constituted collective or institutional identity;
- shared undertaking or public function;
- decision procedures;
- integrated authority and control;
- policies, incentives, resource allocation, or culture;
- coordinated capability and operational process;
- continuity, memory, and successor arrangements;
- legal recognition;
- public representation and answerability; or
- an applicable source that assigns the whole a duty or remedy role.

No item is universally sufficient. Legal entity status can establish a legal bearer within a jurisdiction without establishing moral blame or operational agency. Integrated process can support institutional agency without legal personality.

### 7.8 Distributed allocation

Distributed responsibility applies when relations span a network, supply chain, ecosystem, community, federated system, or other arrangement without one complete center. The analysis should preserve:

- nodes and subject identities;
- connection and dependency types;
- information, authority, and control distribution;
- local and whole-level decisions;
- protocol, standard, market, contractual, or institutional coordination;
- causal and temporal paths;
- opacity, proprietary boundaries, and evidence loss;
- response and remedy reachability; and
- whether the arrangement itself qualifies as a subject for any relation.

The existence of a network does not establish a distributed whole as responsible. It does establish that allocation cannot be reduced to one proximate actor without testing the network.

### 7.9 Hybrid allocation

A Human–artificial hybrid can include Humans, models, agents, interfaces, datasets, policies, infrastructure, organizations, and external authorities. Allocation should identify:

- which subject generated, selected, recommended, decided, approved, authorized, executed, monitored, intervened, corrected, or represented;
- which functions were automated, delegated, constrained, or retained;
- actual information and practical alternatives at each point;
- interface, incentive, dependency, calibration, warning, and supervision effects;
- configured-system or whole-level agency Evidence;
- responsibility sources and bearer eligibility for each relation kind; and
- answerability, correction, remedy, and continuity paths.

“Human in the loop,” “AI agent,” “autonomous system,” or “copilot” is not an allocation.

### 7.10 No responsibility conservation law

Responsibility is not a substance whose quantities must total one. An allocation may contain:

- several full duties of different kinds;
- overlapping answerability;
- joint and individual relations;
- institutional and member-level relations;
- strict legal liability alongside non-legal correction duties;
- remedy responsibility without blame;
- shared causal Contribution with unequal control; and
- unresolved claims under incompatible frames.

Percentages or weights are permitted only if the governing frame defines the relation, denominator, criteria, Evidence, and interpretation. A causal contribution percentage cannot silently become a moral, legal, remedial, or institutional responsibility share.

## 8. Stable-boundary and anti-shopping rules

### 8.1 Boundaries must be declared before conclusion

A material allocation should declare:

- responsibility object and target;
- subject and system boundaries;
- causal model and level;
- Domain, Context, Scope, population, stakes, time, and versions;
- role, authority, authorization, decision, control, and supervision boundaries;
- governing frames and jurisdictions; and
- relevant alternatives and counterfactuals.

These can be revised through Evidence and challenge, but not silently changed to obtain a preferred responsibility conclusion.

### 8.2 Boundary shopping patterns

Boundary shopping includes:

- treating an artificial component as an independent actor when harm occurs but as a provider tool when benefit is claimed;
- treating an institution as a unified whole for authority and success but as unrelated individuals for duty or remedy;
- treating an employee as the organization when assigning blame but not when allocating information, resources, or control;
- treating a provider as outside the deployment while it retains update, information, access, or shutdown control;
- treating a customer as fully responsible while a supplier defines nontransparent defaults and constraints;
- treating a supply chain as distributed when answerability is sought but integrated when market control or value is claimed;
- changing the causal target from decision to final action to exclude upstream roles;
- selecting only the production version for benefit and an obsolete component version for harm;
- using legal entity boundaries to erase operational process, or operational boundaries to erase legal liability; and
- expanding or shrinking the affected population after the outcome.

The response is not to select one permanent boundary. It is to state each relevant level, preserve cross-level relations, justify transitions, and test the conclusion under stable alternatives.

### 8.3 Outcome symmetry

Subject and allocation criteria should not change merely because the outcome is beneficial or harmful. If a configured hybrid is treated as a whole-level agent for success, the same boundary must at least be tested for failure; if an institution claims organizational competence, its integrated processes remain relevant to defect and remedy. This does not guarantee symmetric blame or liability, because source, duty, knowledge, control, and outcome criteria may differ. It prevents opportunistic boundary change.

## 9. Delegation, outsourcing, procurement, and contract

### 9.1 Delegation allocation

A delegation map should preserve:

- delegator and delegate;
- delegated object and relation kind;
- source and authority to delegate;
- Scope, Context, time, conditions, capability, information, resources, and acceptance;
- authority, authorization, control, and practical alternatives supplied;
- supervision, monitoring, escalation, recall, correction, and review;
- transferred and retained duties, answerability, accountability, liability, remedy, and records;
- subdelegation and external-authority constraints; and
- failure, expiry, revocation, succession, and challenge.

Delegation may create new responsibility for the delegate and preserve responsibility for the delegator. It does not require a zero-sum transfer.

### 9.2 Outsourcing

Outsourcing changes organizational location or contract relation. It does not automatically transfer:

- purpose and population selection;
- procurement and supplier-selection responsibility;
- externally imposed duties;
- retained authority, information, access, update, shutdown, or remedy control;
- supervision and assurance duties;
- answerability to affected subjects or authorities;
- historical records and continuity; or
- responsibility for an incapable, under-resourced, opaque, or unchallengeable arrangement.

The contractor can hold its own duties and liability. The customer can retain others. Exact legal force remains external.

### 9.3 Procurement

Procurement can shape responsibility before deployment through:

- purpose and requirement definition;
- supplier and component selection;
- evidence and audit requirements;
- semantic and technical compatibility;
- allocation clauses and insurance;
- disclosure, update, maintenance, incident, and exit terms;
- affected-population and remedy conditions; and
- acceptance, validation, and rejection authority.

A purchaser's inability to inspect every internal detail does not remove deployment and procurement relations. A provider's lack of deployment control does not make it responsible for every downstream use. The allocation must preserve both limits and retained connections.

### 9.4 Contract boundaries

A contract may allocate tasks, costs, risk, liability, indemnity, records, notice, audit, repair, or remedy under its governing law. It cannot by semantic declaration alone:

- erase actual causal Contribution, control, or information;
- bind nonparties or external authorities;
- create informed consent for affected nonparticipants;
- transfer moral blame;
- make an artificial system the sole responsible party;
- make an incapable bearer effective;
- eliminate duties imposed elsewhere; or
- define universal SymK meaning.

Contractual allocation, operational allocation, external legal effect, and the complete responsibility relation remain separate.

## 10. Update, maintenance, replacement, merger, dissolution, and succession

### 10.1 Versioned allocation

Every material allocation should be time- and version-scoped. At minimum distinguish:

- responsibility for original design or collection;
- responsibility for current configuration and deployment;
- responsibility for known defects and warnings;
- update authority and decision responsibility;
- maintenance and monitoring duties;
- responsibility for compatibility, migration, rollback, and decommissioning;
- historical answerability and record custody;
- current correction and remedy capacity; and
- predecessor and successor relations.

The subject responsible for current correction may differ from the subject responsible for creating the defect. Both relations can exist without blame transfer.

### 10.2 Maintenance

Maintenance can include monitoring, repair, data refresh, retraining, patching, validation, documentation, access control, dependency management, incident response, and retirement. “Maintainer” is not one universal role. The allocation must state the maintained object, capability, authority, access, information, resources, Scope, response time, dependencies, and retained provider/deployer relations.

Failure to maintain is not established without an applicable duty and performance criteria. Successful maintenance does not absorb original design, deployment, user, institutional, or external relations.

### 10.3 Component replacement and model update

Replacement or update may change:

- capability, agency, control, and decision paths;
- data, model, configuration, and interface behavior;
- provider, maintainer, operator, or supervisor;
- known limitations, uncertainty, and Evidence;
- authorization, reliance, monitoring, and affected population;
- causal and responsibility relations; and
- remedy or rollback feasibility.

The prior and new versions must remain identifiable. A new version does not inherit every predicate automatically; a continuous service name does not erase the earlier history.

### 10.4 Merger, acquisition, dissolution, and succession

A continuity analysis should identify:

- predecessor and successor subjects and levels;
- legal, contractual, constitutional, organizational, technical, or operational continuity sources;
- assets, systems, people, records, roles, authority, duties, claims, liabilities, remedies, and capabilities transferred or retained;
- known affected parties, open incidents, unresolved challenges, and historical Evidence;
- archive and answerability custody;
- practical remedy and enforcement paths; and
- gaps produced by dissolution, jurisdiction, insolvency, disappearance, or incapacity.

No universal succession rule is accepted. External law may transfer liabilities; organizational governance may transfer maintenance; moral or historical responsibility may remain contested. SymK preserves the map and the gap.

## 11. Collective and institutional responsibility

### 11.1 Whole-level candidate test

A whole-level responsibility claim should identify:

1. whole identity and boundary;
2. source recognizing or constituting the whole-level relation;
3. relevant shared purpose, undertaking, public function, or responsibility object;
4. organization, decision procedure, authority, and control;
5. information, capability, resources, incentives, policies, and practical alternatives;
6. integration among member or component Contributions;
7. continuity, memory, replacement, and succession;
8. representation and answerability;
9. affected-party, correction, remedy, and external-authority interfaces;
10. member/component relations and dissent; and
11. Evidence, uncertainty, assessment, status, and challenge.

No single item proves the claim. The list tests whether the whole is more than a name or aggregate for the relation at issue.

### 11.2 Institutional responsibility

Institutional responsibility can concern:

- constituted duties or public functions;
- policies, budgets, staffing, incentives, training, and resource allocation;
- information and decision architecture;
- procurement, deployment, supervision, and maintenance;
- institutional Knowledge, memory, warning, and Learning;
- repeated or structural patterns not reducible to one episode;
- representation, answerability, correction, remedy, and continuity; or
- legal liability and external obligations.

An institution can be responsible for conditions it organizes even when no single member has complete knowledge, control, or causal sufficiency. That possibility does not prove the relation in a case and does not erase identifiable member responsibilities.

### 11.3 Committee and collective decision

A committee can reach a decision that no member individually proposed or fully endorses. Analysis should preserve:

- authority and mandate;
- agenda and alternatives;
- information and Evidence;
- deliberation and participation;
- voting, consensus, aggregation, delegation, or other decision rule;
- abstention, recusal, dissent, minority report, and suppression;
- final decision status and representative act;
- execution and consequence; and
- whole-level and member-level answerability.

The decision can be attributable to the committee under its procedure without copying the same decision, blame, or liability to every member.

### 11.4 Structural and cultural conditions

Institutional policies, norms, incentives, workload, training, authority gradients, data access, interface design, procurement practices, and challenge protection can shape conduct across many subjects. “Culture” must not become a vague substitute for Evidence. A structural claim should identify mechanisms, levels, persistence, affected processes, alternatives, and relation to specific duties or outcomes.

Structural responsibility does not require that every member intended or knew every effect. Exact moral, legal, and remedial implications remain framework-specific.

## 12. Distributed and hybrid responsibility

### 12.1 Distributed arrangement without a whole-level bearer

A distributed process can contain real local responsibilities even when the arrangement does not qualify as one whole-level bearer. For example:

- each node may govern its data or actions;
- a protocol authority may govern interoperability;
- a platform may govern access or ranking;
- a deployer may govern Context;
- professional users may govern application;
- an institution may govern remedy; and
- external law may assign liability.

The absence of one whole-level responsible subject does not imply a gap if adequate relations and response paths exist. Conversely, many local assignments do not establish complete coverage.

### 12.2 Distributed whole-level bearer

A distributed arrangement may support a whole-level relation if a governing source and Evidence establish the relevant identity, organization, integrated process, decision/control, continuity, representation, and response. Fluid membership, decentralized control, or technical distribution does not automatically defeat the claim, but it raises boundary, authority, Evidence, and answerability challenges.

The burden is relation-specific. A network may qualify for operational agency yet lack legal personality; a consortium may have contractual duties without one integrated Intelligence; a community may provide collective answerability without centralized control.

### 12.3 Hybrid whole and components

A configured Human–artificial system may exhibit whole-level operational agency or capability while responsibility remains distributed through:

- provider design and update relations;
- integrator configuration;
- institutional purpose, resources, and deployment;
- Human expertise, judgment, reliance, and intervention;
- artificial recommendation, decision, execution, monitoring, or control;
- infrastructure and data dependencies;
- external authority; and
- affected-party and remedy pathways.

A whole-level responsibility claim needs its own source and Evidence. It cannot be inferred from capability alone. Component relations persist where relevant; the whole does not absorb them.

### 12.4 Artificial component as operational bearer

An artificial component may be assigned operational responsibility-like functions such as monitoring, maintaining a constraint, requesting escalation, preserving records, or performing correction. The allocation must distinguish:

- assigned function from valid responsibility relation;
- technical permission from authority;
- capability from reliable performance;
- output from answerability;
- operational status from legal or moral status;
- artificial component from provider, deployer, operator, institution, and configured whole; and
- component failure from blame or liability.

If no Human or institution can understand, intervene, answer, correct, or provide remedy, naming the component “responsible” does not close the gap.

## 13. Responsibility-gap diagnosis

### 13.1 Gap predicate

A gap claim should identify:

1. the responsibility object and relation claimed to be needed;
2. source or basis for that need;
3. required bearer or forum conditions;
4. existing candidate bearers and why each is invalid, incapable, unreachable, insufficiently empowered, conflicted, or out of Scope;
5. existing allocations and retained relations;
6. affected subjects and consequences;
7. temporal and jurisdictional boundary;
8. Evidence, uncertainty, and alternative explanations;
9. whether the gap concerns performance, answerability, assessment, correction, enforcement, remedy, or continuity; and
10. challenge, escalation, containment, and unresolved status.

Without these dimensions, “responsibility gap” is an assertion rather than a diagnosis.

### 13.2 Gap types

| Gap type | Working meaning | Typical evidence question |
|---|---|---|
| Semantic | The relation kind, object, or bearer condition is undefined or incoherent | What exact responsibility is said to be missing? |
| Evidentiary | Material facts or connections cannot presently be supported | Which causal, role, control, authority, duty, or consequence Evidence is absent? |
| Attributional | The appropriate subject or level cannot be responsibly identified | Is the issue individual, component, whole, institution, network, successor, or several? |
| Allocative | Applicable relations exist but are not assigned, coordinated, retained, or routed | Which object lacks a valid allocation? |
| Authority | No competent forum can constitute, assess, decide, or route the claim | Who has jurisdiction and practical authority? |
| Capability | A named bearer or forum lacks information, competence, resources, access, control, or time | Can the relation be performed effectively? |
| Enforcement | A recognized relation lacks practical consequence or implementation | Can a decision or duty be made effective? |
| Remedy | Affected subjects lack an accessible and adequate response path | Who can stop, correct, restore, compensate, or repair? |
| Continuity | Duties, records, authority, claims, answerability, or remedies fail across change | What survives update, replacement, dissolution, or succession? |
| Normative conflict | Applicable sources impose incompatible relations without an accepted priority or routing rule | Which sources conflict and who can decide? |

Multiple types may coexist.

### 13.3 Genuine, manufactured, representational, and governance gaps

- **Genuine gap:** after adequate identification, Evidence, allocation, authority, capability, and response analysis, the required relation still has no valid or practically realizable bearer or forum under the governing frames.
- **Manufactured gap:** organizational or technical design, outsourcing, opacity, contractual fragmentation, under-resourcing, assignment to an incapable or unreachable subject, dissolution, automation, interface design, or evidence destruction creates or preserves the absence.
- **Representational gap:** the relation or bearer exists but the model, boundary, status, record, or Projection omits or distorts it.
- **Governance gap:** no adequate source, allocation, authority, accountability, correction, remedy, review, or continuity arrangement was constituted despite the relevant governance claim.

These labels are assessments, not automatic blame judgments. A manufactured or governance gap may itself become a responsibility object under an applicable source, but Stream D assigns no bearer.

### 13.4 No default gap filler

When a gap appears, do not automatically assign it to:

- the nearest Human;
- the final approver;
- the most capable expert;
- the provider;
- the deployer;
- the user;
- the organization;
- the legal entity;
- the artificial component;
- the configured system;
- the collective;
- the deepest-pocketed subject; or
- the affected party.

Each may be relevant under a source. None is the universal residual bearer. The gap remains explicit until adequate allocation, routing, or acknowledged limitation exists.

## 14. Accountability and remedy coverage

### 14.1 Allocation coverage is relation-specific

An allocation can be complete for one purpose and deficient for another. A system may have:

- complete task assignment but no answerability;
- complete legal liability allocation but no practical correction;
- complete causal traceability but no competent forum;
- effective incident response but no affected-party remedy;
- clear individual duties but no institutional resource responsibility;
- institutional answerability but no member-level Evidence; or
- comprehensive records but no challenge or review.

“Complete allocation” must name the object and response horizon.

### 14.2 Effective accountability coverage

For each material responsibility object, test whether there is:

- an identifiable bearer or explicitly unresolved claim;
- an identifiable claimant, forum, reviewer, or routing authority;
- adequate information and Evidence access;
- practical ability and protection to answer and challenge;
- competent assessment and decision authority;
- correction, containment, escalation, or remedy capacity;
- review, appeal, reopening, and external-authority interfaces;
- continuity across role, component, organizational, and version change; and
- a record that preserves rather than manufactures the relations.

This test does not impose a universal duty. It evaluates whether an arrangement claiming accountability actually covers its stated target.

### 14.3 Remedy allocation

The subject able or required to provide remedy may differ from:

- the causal contributor;
- the blameworthy subject;
- the duty bearer who breached;
- the legal entity held liable;
- the current maintainer;
- the insurer or compensation fund;
- the authority ordering relief; and
- the institution capable of prevention.

These relations must not collapse. Stream E determines the minimum affected-party, standing, consent, challenge, and repair boundary; external authority and derived projects determine exact remedies.

## 15. Alternatives considered

### A — Allocate all responsibility to the final decision-maker or approver

**Rejected.** It hides upstream design, data, integration, policy, authority, control, supervision, maintenance, institutional, and remedy relations; it also rewards ceremonial approval and creates a Human responsibility sink.

### B — Allocate responsibility in proportion to causal Contribution

**Rejected.** Causal models are target- and level-relative and do not measure duty, authority, answerability, blame, liability, correction, or remedy. Some contributors are causally material without normative responsibility; some duty bearers are answerable without causal contribution.

### C — Allocate responsibility to whoever has the most control

**Rejected as exhaustive.** Control is material but target-specific and may be split. Responsibility can also arise from role, undertaking, law, answerability, remedy, or institutional source; control can exist without authority or duty.

### D — Allocate responsibility through role or contract fields

**Rejected.** Assignment can be invalid, incomplete, incapable, contradicted by external authority, or strategically used for laundering. Role and contract are sources or records, not complete relations.

### E — Treat responsibility as equal shares among contributors

**Rejected.** Equality ignores relation kinds, Scope, authority, knowledge, control, duty, affected subjects, and response capacity and fabricates an additive denominator.

### F — Assign whole responsibility to the organization and ignore individuals

**Rejected.** Institution-level relations may be real but do not erase member, role, decision, action, dissent, control, or blame evidence.

### G — Reduce organizations to individual responsibility

**Rejected.** It erases whole-level policies, resources, incentives, authority, process, continuity, legal status, structural effects, answerability, and remedy capacity.

### H — Assign residual responsibility to a Human whenever an artificial system is involved

**Rejected.** Human type does not create capability, authority, control, knowledge, or an applicable responsibility source. It incentivizes ceremonial oversight and hides institutional and provider relations.

### I — Assign responsibility to the autonomous or artificial system

**Rejected as a default.** Operational agency or control does not create legal personality, moral standing, blame, liability, or an adequate remedy bearer and must not erase the configured, provider, deployer, operator, institutional, and external-authority history.

### J — Declare distributed complexity a responsibility gap

**Rejected.** Distribution may support real local, overlapping, whole-level, institutional, answerability, or remedial relations. A gap requires object-, source-, bearer-, capability-, forum-, and response-specific diagnosis.

### K — Use a typed, multi-level, non-additive allocation map with stable boundaries, explicit retained relations, temporal continuity, gap diagnosis, and response coverage

**Retained provisionally.** It preserves plural sources and responsibility kinds, prevents property transfer and boundary shopping, supports institutional and hybrid cases, and keeps genuine uncertainty visible without inventing a bearer.

## 18. Three-view preservation obligations

### 18.1 Human View

The Human View should preserve:

- how each subject understood their function, responsibility, authority, dependence, alternatives, and retained relation;
- who believed responsibility was shared, transferred, inherited, rejected, or unresolved;
- actual information, competence, uncertainty, time, resources, incentives, pressure, conflicts, Power, and practical ability to act, refuse, challenge, answer, correct, or repair;
- affected-subject experience, representation, consequences, needs, barriers, remedy, and residual harm;
- institutional narratives, contractual expectations, professional commitments, and disagreement;
- changes through deployment, incident, update, replacement, departure, dissolution, and succession; and
- known gaps between allocation record and lived or practical relation.

Testimony, title, signature, contract, apology, or organizational story is Evidence rather than conclusive allocation.

### 18.2 Scientific View

The Scientific View should preserve, where material:

- causal targets, models, levels, mechanisms, counterfactuals, multiple pathways, dependencies, and uncertainty;
- Evidence concerning agency, control, information, alternatives, role performance, supervision, delegation, organizational effects, and consequences;
- competing individual, component, collective, institutional, distributed, configured-system, and hybrid explanations;
- sensitivity to system, temporal, population, and version boundaries;
- methods for assessing intervention, monitoring, warning, accountability, correction, repair, recurrence, and residual effect;
- sampling, missing data, confounding, selection, survivorship, measurement, and attribution limits; and
- separation of empirical contributions from normative, legal, professional, organizational, and remedial allocations.

No causal score, Shapley value, risk contribution, error rate, audit count, override rate, benchmark, or statistical allocation defines responsibility shares universally.

### 18.3 Engineering View

The Engineering View should eventually preserve:

- every subject, component, role, office, team, institution, legal entity, network, configured system, hybrid, representative, predecessor, successor, authority, claimant, and affected subject as distinct where material;
- responsibility objects and relation kinds separately;
- source, frame, jurisdiction, Domain, Context, Scope, time, version, condition, expiry, succession, status, and Evidence;
- causal, agency, control, role, authority, authorization, decision, delegation, supervision, reliance, dependence, benefit, consequence, and remedy links;
- distinct, sequential, parallel, nested, shared, joint, overlapping, retained, transferred, inherited, vicarious, whole-level, distributed, hybrid, and unresolved allocation claims;
- declared policy, contract, role matrix, configuration, runtime behavior, observed effect, assessment, enforcement, remedy, and record separately;
- information, capability, resources, access, control, override, escalation, rollback, correction, and response capacity;
- challenge, disagreement, affected-party representation, external authority, review, appeal, reopening, and Projection loss; and
- history across procurement, deployment, update, maintenance, replacement, merger, dissolution, succession, and retirement.

This stream selects no graph language, matrix, RACI scheme, legal ontology, causal allocation method, responsibility score, case-management system, identity model, workflow, contract template, audit platform, accountability metric, remedy process, or implementation.

### 18.4 Alignment rule

A Human narrative saying “the organization owns it,” a Scientific analysis assigning causal influence to several components, and an Engineering matrix naming one accountable approver may all be locally accurate but semantically misaligned if they concern different objects, levels, periods, or relation kinds. Alignment requires explicit mapping, not forced equality. Material missing control, incapable ownership, omitted affected subjects, unrepresented retention, or absent response paths keeps the allocation challenged.

## 20. Consequences for governing artifacts

### Product Vision

The Product Vision's complete value chain requires lifecycle allocation rather than final-output ownership. Better answers must preserve who sourced, designed, integrated, assessed, decided, authorized, deployed, relied, acted, supervised, maintained, challenged, was affected, corrected, and responded. Distribution cannot become an excuse for absent answerability or remedy, but the Product Vision does not itself assign actual bearers.

### DR-009 through DR-013

- DR-009's distinction among foundational meaning, normative duty, and implementation prevents an allocation model from becoming a hidden responsibility policy.
- DR-010 supplies subject Identity, Context, Scope, Domain, Relationship, Applicability, Bearer, Representation, Projection, and System boundaries.
- DR-011 prevents Intelligence, capability, performance, Bearer attribution, or whole-level profile from manufacturing responsibility.
- DR-012 supplies Evidence, Provenance, assessment, status, reliance, recursive challenge, consequence return, and three-view alignment for allocation claims.
- DR-013 supplies Participant/Contribution, cooperative-unit, Power, challenge, affected-party, Persistence, member change, memory, replacement, system-Learning, and succession boundaries without making Cooperation responsible by occurrence.

No predecessor is reopened.

### Candidate A0

Responsible Cooperation cannot mean that every Participant shares equal responsibility or that the cooperative unit absorbs member duties. A0 must preserve shared undertaking, Power, affected subjects, role/authority asymmetry, whole/member relations, retained responsibility, challenge, correction, and remedy without turning responsibility into a prerequisite for Cooperation occurrence.

### Candidate A1

- allocate explicit responsibility objects and relation kinds, not an undifferentiated responsibility field;

- require subject and level Evidence;
- permit distinct, overlapping, shared, joint, institutional, distributed, and hybrid relations without equality or additivity;
