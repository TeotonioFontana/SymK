# Discovery Viability Analysis

## Purpose

This document defines a **viability analysis method** for early product evaluation.

Its purpose is to help kill weak bets early, before they consume large amounts of:

- design effort,
- engineering time,
- founder attention,
- market goodwill,
- and strategic focus.

This method belongs in the **Discovery Phase**.

It should be used before architecture enthusiasm, branding enthusiasm, or AI enthusiasm begin to distort judgment.

---

## Core Principle

A product idea is not attractive because it is interesting.

A product idea is attractive only when there is a credible intersection of:

- real pain,
- reachable buyers,
- visible spend,
- a defendable wedge,
- and a plausible route to traction.

The job of viability analysis is to test whether that intersection exists.

---

## What the analysis should answer

A proper viability analysis should answer the following questions:

1. Is the problem real?
2. Is the problem painful enough?
3. Are there buyers with budget?
4. Is the market already crowded?
5. Is there a strong entry wedge?
6. Do we have an unfair advantage?
7. Can we build something credible fast enough?
8. Can the product become strategically important?
9. Can the product be reused across a broader SaaS portfolio?
10. Is the expected probability of success high enough to deserve focus?

---

## Recommended Analysis Dimensions

## 1. Problem Intensity

### Question
How painful is the problem?

### What to look for
- repeated complaints,
- expensive manual work,
- operational friction,
- regulatory risk,
- time waste,
- quality loss,
- cognitive overload,
- revenue leakage,
- customer dissatisfaction.

### Warning sign
If the problem is annoying but not expensive, urgent, or repeated, it may not support a strong product.

---

## 2. Frequency

### Question
How often does the problem occur?

### What to look for
- daily workflows,
- repeated professional routines,
- recurring bottlenecks,
- constant review burden,
- repeated document production,
- repeated decision or intake steps.

### Warning sign
A painful problem that happens rarely may not create a strong software category.

---

## 3. Buyer and Budget Evidence

### Question
Are there real buyers with real budget?

### What to look for
- existing paid tools,
- software categories with active spend,
- procurement patterns,
- consulting spend around the problem,
- clear budget owners,
- willingness to pay for efficiency, risk reduction, or revenue gain.

### Warning sign
A strong user pain without a clear budget owner can become a difficult commercial problem.

---

## 4. Competitive Density

### Question
How crowded is the market?

### What to look for
- number of direct competitors,
- maturity of existing players,
- price transparency,
- product quality,
- feature overlap,
- strength of brand or distribution,
- enterprise lock-in.

### Warning sign
If the market is crowded and the new product has no strong wedge, the idea may be strategically weak.

---

## 5. Entry Wedge

### Question
Why would this product win initial traction?

### What to look for
- ignored niche,
- underserved geography,
- language gap,
- weak incumbent UX,
- poor institutional-memory support,
- weak compliance/governance,
- missing integration layer,
- cross-domain reuse,
- lower-friction adoption.

### Warning sign
If the product can only enter by being “slightly better,” the wedge is weak.

---

## 6. Founder Unfair Advantage

### Question
Why are we unusually suited to pursue this product?

### What to look for
- direct customer access,
- insider workflow knowledge,
- existing client relationships,
- credibility in the sector,
- prior infrastructure,
- reusable architecture,
- domain-specific insight,
- operational proximity.

### Warning sign
If the team has no distribution edge, no customer access, and no domain insight, the probability of traction falls sharply.

---

## 7. Compliance and Regulatory Friction

### Question
How much regulatory and governance burden exists?

### What to look for
- privacy requirements,
- auditability needs,
- human-review obligations,
- professional liability,
- data retention constraints,
- explainability pressure,
- ethical constraints,
- approval-chain complexity.

### Warning sign
High regulation is not necessarily bad, but it raises cost, slows iteration, and favors stronger execution.

---

## 8. Speed to Credible MVP

### Question
How quickly can a believable first product be built?

### What to look for
- access to enabling AI/speech/retrieval tools,
- complexity of core workflow,
- integration burden,
- availability of pilot users,
- need for domain tuning,
- dependency on third parties,
- required governance features.

### Warning sign
If the MVP takes too long, the learning loop becomes expensive and slow.

---

## 9. Distribution Feasibility

### Question
Can the product reach customers efficiently?

### What to look for
- existing relationships,
- communities,
- referral channels,
- consultants or influencers,
- marketplaces,
- embedded/partner channels,
- direct enterprise path,
- product-led trial path.

### Warning sign
A good product without a realistic path to customers is still a weak bet.

---

## 10. Reusability Across Portfolio

### Question
Can this product create assets reusable across other SaaS products?

### What to look for
- reusable workflow engine,
- domain-pack architecture,
- shared agentic layer,
- shared institutional-memory model,
- common document engine,
- common review/governance layer,
- common transcription/relevance framework.

### Warning sign
A product that cannot create reusable strategic assets may still win, but it is less valuable for building a SaaS portfolio.

---

## Recommended Scoring Model

Each analysis dimension can be scored from **1 to 5**.

### Positive dimensions
- problem intensity
- frequency
- buyer/budget evidence
- entry wedge
- founder unfair advantage
- distribution feasibility
- portfolio reusability

### Negative or burden dimensions
- competitive density
- compliance friction
- time to credible MVP

---

## Suggested Formula

A simple working formula is:

**Viability Score = (Pain + Frequency + Budget + Wedge + Founder Fit + Distribution + Reuse) - (Crowding + Regulation + Time-to-MVP)**

This formula should not be treated as mathematical truth.

Its purpose is to force disciplined comparison between opportunities.

---

## Decision Thresholds

## High viability
Characteristics:
- clear pain,
- repeated workflow,
- visible buyer budget,
- reachable customers,
- meaningful wedge,
- manageable build path,
- strategic reuse value.

### Suggested action
Advance to deeper Discovery or pilot planning.

---

## Medium viability
Characteristics:
- some visible pain,
- some buyer signal,
- but weak wedge, weak distribution, or uncertain speed to MVP.

### Suggested action
Keep under observation, test with narrow research, or defer.

---

## Low viability
Characteristics:
- unclear pain,
- weak budget signal,
- crowded market,
- no unfair advantage,
- slow MVP path,
- no strategic reuse.

### Suggested action
Kill early.

---

## Recommended Discovery Workflow

For each candidate product:

1. Define the problem clearly.
2. Identify the buyer and user separately.
3. Map the current workaround.
4. Estimate pain, frequency, and spend.
5. Map direct and adjacent competitors.
6. Define the entry wedge.
7. Identify founder unfair advantage.
8. Estimate compliance and build burden.
9. Estimate distribution feasibility.
10. Score reusability across the portfolio.
11. Assign a viability score.
12. Decide:
   - advance,
   - defer,
   - or kill.

---

## Recommended Output Format

Each Discovery viability record should include:

- Product concept
- Target market
- User
- Buyer
- Core problem
- Current workaround
- Pain evidence
- Spend evidence
- Competitor map
- Entry wedge
- Founder fit
- Compliance burden
- MVP path
- Distribution path
- Portfolio reuse value
- Final viability score
- Recommendation

---

## Important Rule

Do not allow enthusiasm for:

- AI,
- branding,
- technical elegance,
- or personal fascination

to substitute for viability evidence.

A product should survive Discovery because it has a credible path to traction, not because it sounds intelligent.

---

## Final Principle

The purpose of Discovery viability analysis is not to prove that an idea is brilliant.

The purpose is to determine whether the idea deserves the next level of attention.

A strong Discovery process should reduce waste, protect focus, and improve the probability that only high-quality bets move forward.
